## Marketing — Comedian Promo Stunts — DECIDED
- Original plan: pay professional comedians to promote the app, including secretly roasting unsuspecting regular users as a promo stunt (they don't know in advance, get surprised by a "master" roaster).
- **DECIDED to keep this tactic, WITH two guardrails**:
  1. **Specific post-match consent from the surprised user** before any footage from that particular stunt is posted publicly — separate from standard recording consent, since being used as a marketing prop is a different situation from an ordinary match. If they decline, the clip stays private/unpublished.
  2. **Public disclosure/labeling when posted** — e.g. "Special Guest Battle" or naming the comedian — rather than presenting it as an ordinary random match. Rationale: presenting a staged encounter as fully organic risks deceptive-advertising concerns (same principle behind FTC sponsored-content disclosure rules) — the AUDIENCE watching the posted clip deserves to know it wasn't a random encounter, separate from the participant's own consent.
- **Action item — NEW**: ask William if he would promote the app on Kill Tony, and offer him some money for the promotion.
- **Founder-led commercials — NEW, DECIDED (developer's own idea)**: make ad creative where the DEVELOPER personally battles someone and roasts them hard — the ad IS a real battle, not an actor demonstrating an app. Strong fit for this product for reasons worth recording: (1) it shows the actual product rather than describing it, which is exactly what performs on TikTok, the primary channel; (2) it's the cheapest possible creative — no talent fee, no script, and the existing highlight-render pipeline already produces the vertical clip; (3) it's honest about what the app is, which pre-qualifies installs and matters for an 18+ app with an explicit free-speech content policy; (4) founder-led content is a proven cold-start pattern where the founder is willing to be the face and take the hits.
  - **These are still ADS, so they cross into a different legal regime than in-app content.** The comedian-promo-stunt guardrails already decided above apply here with equal force: **specific consent from the opponent before any footage is published** (being cast in someone's advertising is a materially different ask from playing a match), and **disclosure that this is the app's own promotional material**, not an organically-surfaced random battle — the FTC-disclosure reasoning already recorded for the comedian stunts is, if anything, stronger when the person posting is the owner. Also note these clips face the **platform-policy screening** step (see Auto-Editing for Highlights): a roast that's fine inside the app can still get the marketing account restricted by TikTok/IG/YouTube's own rules, and losing the primary distribution channel to a joke would be a self-inflicted wound.
  - **Natural production slot**: film these during Sixes and Sevens, when the developer is already online seeding matches — same session produces both matchmaking liquidity and ad creative.
  - Not yet designed: how many, cadence, whether opponents are recruited friends or genuinely random, and whether they run as paid spend or organic posts.
- **Paid ambassador/referral push — NEW, DECIDED**: at launch, run a CASH-funded ambassador/referral program (e.g. paying campus or community ambassadors per referral/download+review) — separate from and in addition to the existing in-app points-based referral system. Inspired directly by BeReal's early growth playbook, which paid students per referral and per download-plus-review specifically to seed cold-start growth before organic virality kicked in. Exact payout structure/target communities not yet designed — same "campus/community ambassador" model is a reasonable starting point given target demo (college students) already identified in original marketing notes.

Reachable from a help icon in `HomeScreen`'s app bar, and separately from `BannedScreen`'s app bar too - a banned account can still reach it (the Firestore rule doesn't check `accountStatus`) since a support need (billing, a genuine account issue) can be unrelated to the ban itself and shouldn't be limited to only the ban-appeal channel.
- **Soft launch — DECIDED**: private beta with a small group (friends, via TestFlight for iOS / Play Console internal testing track for Android) BEFORE the full public marketing blitz — catches bugs and lets core mechanics get real playtesting (round length, points balance, matchmaking, etc.) while the stakes and audience are still small. Marketing spend should not go out until the private beta has validated the core loop works. **DECIDED**: ratings/progress from the beta do NOT carry over — everyone resets to fresh 1200 at public launch (beta is purely for testing/bug-catching, not an early-access head start).

## Product Overview
A mobile app (Flutter, Android-first, iOS later) where users are randomly matched 1-on-1 for live 1v1 video roast battles. Winner is determined by community vote (skill-based, not chance). Competitive ranking system with tiers, tournaments, and prizes. Long-term goal: viral growth to 1M+ users, monetized via paid tournament entry fees.

Developer: solo, no prior backend experience, comfortable with cross-platform frameworks, working on Windows, low/no budget for V1. Based in San Diego, CA.

## Core Match Flow
- **Account creation**: phone number + email + password (encrypted). Age-gated via Google Play Age Signals API (see Age Verification below) — no manual ID verification for V1.
- **Onboarding tutorial — BUILT.** `TutorialScreen` (`lib/screens/onboarding/tutorial_screen.dart`), gated in front of a first match by `_ensureTutorialCompleted` in `home_screen.dart` and recorded via `tutorialCompleted` on the user document.
  - **It is an actual practice round, not slides** — the decision specifies "learn by doing", so it runs the real turn machinery (countdown → a timed turn with a live clock and the End My Turn button → a simulated opponent turn showing the mic muted) rather than describing it. The muting rule gets its own beat because it's the one people most often break in a first real match.
  - **It costs nothing to run.** The camera preview uses the video service's local preview WITHOUT joining a channel — Agora bills per participant-minute in a channel and nobody joins one here. That matters because *every* new user passes through this screen, so a tutorial that burned real video minutes would scale its cost with signups rather than with matches actually played. The opponent is simulated for the same reason, and because a first-timer should never be someone else's opponent while they're still working out which way to hold the phone.
  - **Gated at the moment it's relevant** — checked when starting a match rather than on Home, so someone browsing the leaderboard isn't made to sit through it. **Fails open**: if the flag can't be read, the match proceeds. A transient Firestore read shouldn't stop someone playing, and occasionally skipping the tutorial is far cheaper than blocking matches. Completion is a plain field write, not a Cloud Function — this is a UX gate, not a security boundary; nothing is protected by having watched it.
  - **Verified on device**: the gate fires for an un-onboarded account, the live camera and framing oval render, the practice turn counts down with a working End My Turn, and after completion the flow hands straight off to Recording Consent. Re-running a match afterwards goes **directly** to consent, confirming the one-time gate holds.
  - Original decision: mandatory before a user's first match (covers camera positioning/framing AND ground rules — mic muting, round timing, recording consent). One-time gate only — never replayed for returning users after first completion. **Format — DECIDED**: interactive practice round (not a passive video or slides) — user actually walks through a mock match flow to learn by doing.
- **Pre-match check**: camera preview with face-position guide ("hologram" framing overlay), prompts for good lighting/steady camera/loud enough audio. Both users must pass this before match starts.
  - **Build Order step 3 implementation status**: `PreMatchScreen` built with real camera preview + oval framing guide overlay + a REAL mic-level check (gates the "I'm Ready" button — via Agora's `enableAudioVolumeIndication`, only reports once joined to a channel). Lighting/steadiness/face-visible are advisory-only static prompts, NOT auto-detected. **CORRECTION (found during step 9a video-moderation research)**: this section previously claimed `registerVideoFrameObserver` was an unimplemented stub - that was WRONG, based on reading only the auto-generated binding file (`lib/src/binding/agora_media_engine_impl.dart`, which does contain a literal `throw UnimplementedError`) without checking that `RtcEngine.getMediaEngine()` actually returns a DIFFERENT, hand-written override class (`lib/src/impl/agora_media_engine_impl_override.dart`) with a real, working implementation wired to native code. Confirmed live on-device: registering a `VideoFrameObserver` and calling it before `joinChannel` (per Agora's docs) delivered continuous real `onCaptureVideoFrame` callbacks (3000+ frames, consistent 576×1024 I420 buffers) - this is NOT a blocker. See the Content Policy & Moderation section's step 9a status note for what this unblocks.
  - Recording Consent (see Legal Items below) is a separate screen shown BEFORE the pre-match camera check, per the "distinct affirmative action" legal requirement.
- **Auto quality flags**: AI/heuristic detection for too-dark, too-quiet, too-shaky, face not visible, mic muted, connection issues. Flagged matches are auto-cancelled and re-queued — NO score penalty either way, just a re-match prompt. This is a technical quality gate only, not content moderation. **Not yet implemented** — the pre-match mic check above is a one-time gate before a match starts, not the same thing as continuous in-match quality monitoring; the dark/shaky/face-visible detection gap noted above applies here too.
- **Quality-flag abuse safeguard — NEW, DECIDED**: since a disqualified match has no rating penalty and simply triggers a rematch, this creates a potential dodge mechanism (deliberately faking bad lighting/audio to escape a match that's going badly). DECIDED: add monitoring/safeguard against this — e.g., track/flag accounts with abnormally high disqualification-triggering rates for review, rather than treating every disqualification as automatically legitimate. Exact detection mechanism/threshold not yet designed — same theme as other abuse-prevention backlog items (vote fraud, multi-account collusion).
- **Match structure — DECIDED**: Random 1v1 pairing, same language only (English only for V1). **Standard format: 3 rounds, 15 seconds max per turn, used for BOTH exhibition and ranked modes** (tournament format may differ — TBD, could be longer for higher stakes).
- **Live match configuration — BUILT.** Round count, turn length, countdown and bio-reveal length are read from the `config/matchSettings` Firestore document (`functions/matchSettings.js`), not hardcoded — the developer can retune the format live, without shipping a new app version, which was the explicit requirement.
  - **Chose a Firestore document over Firebase Remote Config**, which this doc's schema explicitly permitted ("Remote Config or Firestore doc"). Three reasons, in order of importance: (1) settings are resolved **server-side once, at pairing time, and stamped onto the match document**, so both players provably run identical timings — with client-side Remote Config two devices can hold different cached values and the two halves of a match would silently disagree about how long a turn lasts, a bug that surfaces as "the timer felt wrong" rather than as an error; (2) no new Flutter dependency, so no risk to the AGP/KGP pin this doc documents as fragile; (3) editing it is the same "admin uses the Firebase console" workflow already used for profile approval, report review and tournament creation.
  - **Bounds are enforced server-side because this config is hand-edited in a console against a live app, with no validation layer in between.** A `roundCount` of 0 would end matches before they began; a `roundLengthSeconds` of 9999 would strand two people in a single turn for nearly three hours. Out-of-range or non-numeric values fall back **per field** — first to the top-level value, then to the documented default — so one bad override never discards the rest of a good configuration.
  - **Verified end-to-end against the deployed backend**: non-default values reach a real pairing and are stamped on the match document; both players receive byte-identical settings; a per-mode override applies to that mode only and leaves others untouched; `roundCount: 0` and `roundLengthSeconds: 9999` are both refused in favour of defaults. Plus 13 local checks on the merge/bounds rules. **Confirmed on a real device**: with `bioRevealSeconds` set to 20, the bio reveal counted down from 20 rather than the 60-second default.
  - `firestore.rules` allows any signed-in client to read `config/**` but **nobody** to write it — a client that could edit this would set its own turn length.
- **In-turn countdown timer — NEW, DECIDED**: a visible countdown timer during each player's turn (counting down the 15-second max). UI requirement: small, unobtrusive — should not take up significant screen space or distract from the video feed itself.
  - **MUST be a remote/backend-configurable value (e.g., Firebase Remote Config), NOT hardcoded** — developer wants to tune round count/length post-launch without shipping a new app version, to test what format performs best.
  - Each round: one player gets up to the max time to roast (can end turn early, no penalty for time used or joke count). Opponent's mic is muted during that turn.
  - Between turns: "Player X, get ready" screen + 5-second countdown, then roles swap.
  - After all rounds: both mics unmute (volume slightly lowered) while verdict is delivered.
  - Round format should be mode-based (exhibition/ranked/tournament each get one standardized format), NOT user-customizable per match — customization would fragment ranking data and complicate matchmaking.
  - **Build Order step 4 implementation status**: `MatchScreen` built with a real round/turn/countdown state machine, tested live across two devices. Key implementation notes:
    - **Live configuration is now BUILT** — round count, turn length, countdown and bio-reveal length all come from `config/matchSettings` rather than hardcoded constants, satisfying the "must be remotely configurable, NOT hardcoded" requirement above. See the dedicated note under the Firestore Data Model's `config/matchSettings` entry.
    - **Real matchmaking IS now built** (see the dedicated Matchmaking Queue Architecture status note below) — the hardcoded "test-channel" both devices used to join is gone, and each match gets its own server-assigned channel derived from a real `matches/{matchId}` document. `MatchScreen` now takes a `MatchPairing` (matchId, channelName, opponentId, mode) rather than assuming a shared test room.
    - **Turn sync is real, not simulated**: one device is elected "host" (lower Agora-assigned uid) and drives the authoritative timer, broadcasting round/turn/phase state to the other over Agora's data-stream messaging (`sendStreamMessage`/`onStreamMessage`) — there's no server-authoritative state (no Firestore match doc yet), so this is a peer-to-peer stopgap. Verified live: both devices showed correctly complementary turn banners ("Your turn coming up" vs. "Opponent's turn coming up") with synced countdown numbers, and both reached the verdict screen simultaneously.
    - **Who goes first each round is always the host** — not a documented product decision, just this implementation's default. Needs a real decision (alternate starting player? coin-flip?) before this is production behavior.
    - **Verdict screen is a placeholder** ("Match complete!" + button back to Home) — community voting (Build Order step 5) isn't built yet.
    - **Known unresolved oddity**: during live testing, the mic mute/unmute log showed 12 state transitions for what should be 13 (12 turn-phase transitions + 1 verdict transition) — the expected mute-state flip going into the final phase didn't show up as a distinct log entry. The visible match behavior (banners, countdowns, both devices reaching verdict together) looked correct, so this weakly suggests a harmless dedup/logging artifact rather than a skipped turn, but this was NOT conclusively root-caused. Worth re-verifying with explicit turnIndex/phase logging before relying on this state machine for real matches.
    - A real bug WAS found and fixed during this work: `AgoraVideoCallService.dispose()` must `leaveChannel()` before `release()`, and the caller (`PreMatchScreen`) must `await` that full dispose BEFORE navigating to the next screen — `State.dispose()` can't be awaited by the Flutter framework, so relying on it to clean up before the next screen's engine joins the same channel is a race that Agora loses with `AgoraRtcException(-17, ERR_JOIN_CHANNEL_REJECTED)`.
- **Pre-match bio reveal — BUILT.** `BioRevealScreen` (`lib/screens/match/bio_reveal_screen.dart`) sits between matchmaking and `MatchScreen`, showing the opponent's profile fields plus their volunteered `ammoText` in its own highlighted card. Rank/rating is deliberately absent, per the decision below. Ends after 60s **or** as soon as both players tap Ready, whichever comes first — readiness goes through a `setMatchReady` Cloud Function (clients can't write the matches collection at all, and readiness has to be attributable so a client can't mark its *opponent* ready), and both devices learn about it by listening to the match document. A player whose profile is empty renders an explicit "left their profile blank, you're on your own" rather than a broken-looking empty screen. **Skip/decline is built alongside it**: `skipMatch` spends one of a documented **3 skips per day**, settles the match as `abandoned` + `voteFinalized` so it's never rated or voted on, and records `skippedByUserId` so the *other* player's listener can tell them their opponent passed. The counter (`skipsUsedToday`/`skipsResetDate`) is protected in `firestore.rules` exactly like `rating` — a client that could reset its own counter would have unlimited skips, which is precisely the opponent-cherry-picking the limit exists to prevent. Reset is keyed to the **UTC** day, not the player's local midnight, since no per-user timezone is stored — practical effect is the allowance resets at 5pm Pacific; fine for V1, worth revisiting if players notice. Verified end-to-end against the deployed backend (readiness accumulates and is idempotent; skipping settles the match, spends exactly one skip, blocks a second skip and any vote; the allowance runs out, refuses further skips, and is per-account), plus 6 more local checks covering the day-rollover reset. **Reveal length is live-configurable** via `config/matchSettings`'s `bioRevealSeconds` (see the Live match configuration note above), resolved server-side at pairing time so both players count down together.
- **Original decision, for reference**: after matchmaking pairs two users, show each user the opponent's profile bio (see User Profile System below) for up to 1 minute OR until both players tap "ready," whichever comes first. This gives roasters concrete material ("ammo") instead of relying purely on improv about a stranger's appearance. Full bio is shown pre-match only — NOT persistently visible during the actual roast (keeps video UI clean; also tests what the roaster actually remembers/uses). Optional future iteration: a small persistent one-line tag (name + profession only) pinned in a corner during the match — not required for V1, worth playtesting first. **DECIDED**: rank/rating is NOT shown during bio reveal — only the profile ammo fields (profession, interests, etc). Keeps focus on comedy material rather than skill intimidation/sandbagging psychology.
- **Skip/decline — BUILT** (see the Pre-match bio reveal entry above for the implementation, since the two ship together). Original decision: users may skip a proposed match after seeing the opponent's bio, before it starts. Limited to 2-3 skips per day (prevents cherry-picking "easy" opponents while still giving an escape hatch for a bad pairing); 3/day was chosen from that range.
  - **Auto-requeue after being skipped — BUILT.** The player who *didn't* skip is now put straight back into the queue for the same mode rather than dumped on Home. Being declined is already a small rejection; making them re-navigate consent and the camera check to try again compounds it for no reason, and they passed those checks moments earlier.
- **Match-found notification — BUILT (push half).** `PushNotificationService` (`lib/core/services/push_notification_service.dart`) requests notification permission, stores this device's FCM token in `users/{uid}.fcmTokens` (an **array**, since one account can be signed in on a phone and a tablet per the Device support decision), keeps it current via `onTokenRefresh`, and removes it on sign-out so the next person on that device doesn't get the previous account's alerts. Registration happens in `_AccountStatusGate`, which is now keyed by uid so switching accounts builds fresh state instead of reusing the old element and never registering. Server-side, `pollMatchmaking` calls `notifyMatchFound` after a pairing commits, sending **only to the opponent** — whoever's poll made the pairing is demonstrably in the foreground and already knows. Dead tokens reported by FCM (`registration-token-not-registered`/`invalid-registration-token`) are pruned as they're discovered, so they don't accumulate forever. The whole path is best-effort by design: a denied permission, a missing Play Services, or a failed send never blocks a pairing or a match.
  - **Why this matters more than it looks**: pairing is discovered by client polling, and those timers stall once the app is backgrounded. Without the push, someone who queues and switches away has no way of ever learning they were paired. Their queue entry stays flagged `matched` server-side until collected, so returning to the app at any point still lands them in the right match.
  - **Verified live end-to-end**: a real device registered its token (confirmed present in Firestore), queued through the app, was **backgrounded**, and a second account's poll paired with it — the device received "Opponent found / MMTestA is ready to battle. Tap to start." in the notification shade, confirmed both in `dumpsys notification` and visually.
  - **Toolchain note**: adding `firebase_messaging` did **not** disturb the pinned AGP 8.9.1 / KGP 2.1.0 setup — checked with a real build before any code was written, given how much trouble `image_picker` and `permission_handler` caused before it. `POST_NOTIFICATIONS` was added to the manifest; without it Android 13+ delivers the message but silently never displays it, which looks exactly like a broken backend.
  - **The cold-start recovery hole this opened is now CLOSED.** Tapping the notification opens the app rather than deep-linking, which is fine while the app is merely backgrounded (the matchmaking screen resumes and its next poll finds the match) — but if the process had been killed, the player landed on Home with a live `matched` queue entry and no UI path to reach it. A new `getActiveMatch` Cloud Function scans every mode's queue for the caller's entry, and a `_ActiveMatchBanner` on `HomeScreen` surfaces "You have a match waiting / Rejoin match" when one exists, rendering nothing otherwise. It re-checks on app resume as well as first build, and self-heals: if the match has since ended (opponent skipped, or the sweep abandoned it) the dead queue entry is cleared rather than handed back. This works precisely because matched entries are exempt from stale-entry pruning. It also serves as the **in-app indicator** half of this decision, though only in the recovery position — a live indicator while actively queueing still isn't built.
    - **Verified live in exactly the scenario it exists for**: the device queued through the app, its process was force-stopped, a second account's poll paired with the now-dead client, and on relaunch Home showed the banner; tapping Rejoin landed in the bio reveal against the right opponent with a fresh timer.
  - **Known gaps**: notifications land on FCM's `fcm_fallback_notification_channel` rather than a properly-named channel (shows as "Miscellaneous" in system settings) — declaring a real one needs native channel creation. Per-category mute toggles are still unbuilt — the category (`match_found`) is already in the message's data payload so a preference check can be added server-side without touching the client.
  - Original decision: both push notification AND in-app indicator when an opponent is found. Important given there's no scheduled "battle window" system — relies on marketing-driven concurrent usage, so users need to be pulled back into the app promptly when a match is ready.
- **Daily "prime time" notification — DECIDED AND NAMED: "Sixes and Sevens," 6:00–7:00pm PACIFIC, one global moment.** Originally a vague "daily optional push nudging concurrent usage at a consistent time," inspired by BeReal's daily-notification habit loop. Now specified, because it turns out to be the single highest-leverage fix for the cold-start liquidity problem (see the Async Matchmaking Revision section below) rather than just a retention nudge. Still NOT a mandatory window — matchmaking stays open 24/7 — but everything points at the hour: the push, the points multiplier, and eventually tournament start times.
  - **Why concentrating demand in time beats matching across time**: with users spread over a week and several timezones, effective concurrency is roughly (active users ÷ 168 hours). Nudging the same people into one nightly hour multiplies that by more than an order of magnitude, for free, with no new matchmaking mechanics. This is the difference between "random pairing works" and "random pairing never finds anyone."
  - **ONE global moment, anchored to Pacific — explicitly NOT each user's local 6-7pm.** Local firing was proposed first (so the "6-7" name would be literally true for everyone) and **rejected by the developer, correctly**: it fragments the pool into a separate small queue per timezone, which defeats the entire purpose of having a window. Uniformity beats locality. 6-7pm Pacific puts the whole continental US in a good evening slot (7-8 Mountain, 8-9 Central, 9-10 Eastern — the East Coast lands on the single best social hour of the day), which covers the beta (San Diego friends) and the stated target demo (US college students).
  - **The honest cost is international**: 2-3am UK, late morning in Australia. Accepted for now — there are no UK/AU users yet. **When a region grows enough to sustain its own pool, add a SECOND anchored window (e.g. "6-7pm GMT"), NOT a rolling local-time roll.** Two or three fixed moments worldwide, each internally uniform, each keeping the 6-7 name. That's the scaling answer; don't re-litigate it as "local time" later.
  - **Naming — "Sixes and Sevens" is the product name, "6-7" is the shorthand.** The time is deliberately coupled to the "6-7" meme because it makes the window *self-explaining* — the hardest part of any scheduled event is getting people to remember when it is, and that number is already lodged in the target demo's head. But the meme is NOT the product name, for two reasons: (1) meme decay — tying a permanent feature to a trend that peaked in late 2025 means it reads as dated within a year; (2) **and this is the serious one — "6-7" is strongly associated with middle schoolers ("6-7 kids" is literally the derisive term for that age group). This app is 18+ hard-gated, and per the App Store Compliance and Random-Stranger-Video-Pairing Design Risk sections, an 18+ random-video-chat app whose flagship daily event is named after a Gen Alpha meme is a genuinely bad look in front of a store reviewer assessing whether it markets to minors.** "Sixes and Sevens" is a real English idiom meaning chaos/disarray, contains the numbers, gets the wink from anyone who knows the trend, and ages fine when the trend dies. The shorthand "6-7" is what goes in TikTok captions and push copy.
  - **Runs a clean 6:00–7:00pm.** A 67-minute window (6:00–7:07pm) was proposed as a community in-joke — an arbitrary precise rule being a better shibboleth than an earnest one — and **the developer rejected it as probably less fun in practice than it sounds on paper.** Agreed: it's the kind of detail that delights the person who invented it and confuses everyone else, and "6-7" is doing the memorability work already. Revisitable, but the clean hour is the default.

  - **WHAT COUNTS AS A WINDOW MATCH — DECIDED AND BUILT, before any reward was attached to it.** Qualification is judged at the **START** of a match, never at the end: a battle that kicks off at 6:58 and runs past 7:00 qualifies in full. There is deliberately **no partial credit and no proration**.

Matchmaking latency is not the player's fault, and losing a bonus to it is the kind of arbitrary penalty that makes a reward feel rigged. The grace is **bounded rather than open-ended** specifically because the planned standing-challenge design lets queue entries live for hours - without a cap, a challenge left at 6:59pm would still be minting bonuses at midnight.

A match is judged by the rules in force when it was played, so retiming, renaming or disabling the window from the console never retroactively rewrites history.     - **Keyed by the Pacific day of the upcoming window, not the calendar date or the viewer's local date.** Two separate reasons, both load-bearing: a commitment made at 8pm must book **tomorrow** rather than an evening that has already happened, and a viewer in Sydney whose local calendar already reads tomorrow must still commit to the same night as everyone else — otherwise the count splits across two keys and both look emptier than the truth. Implemented on both sides (`upcomingWindowDayKey` in `functions/eventWindow.js` and `lib/core/services/event_window.dart`) with **one-for-one mirrored tests**, because if they drift someone commits to one night and is counted for another and nothing would surface it.

It uses an aggregation `count()` rather than reading the matching documents, which stays cheap precisely as the number grows. There's nothing to protect: the only thing a modified client could do is commit itself, which it can do legitimately anyway, and the count can't be inflated beyond one per account. Keeping it a direct write means the button responds instantly with no cold start.

The instinct is right — the judges already exist during that hour and a 24-hour window spends them on nothing — but two problems stopped it being built now:
    1. **It could make the hour count for LESS, inverting the intent.** Rating scales by how much judgement a match actually received, so a 30-minute window only beats 24 hours if half an hour of Sixes and Sevens out-produces a quiet day. At beta volume it very likely will not, which would make the matches most meant to matter the thinnest-judged in the app.

Same live-event effect during the hour, no thin-result risk, self-adjusting as the userbase grows, and it is one rule rather than a special case for window matches. - **The Sixes and Sevens bonus is a POINTS multiplier, NOT a rating multiplier — decided explicitly, after the question was raised.** Points and rating are separate systems (see Ranking System): points only ever increase and buy cosmetics, rating goes up and down and drives rank. Doubling points during the window is pure carrot with no downside risk. **Doubling RATING during the window was considered and rejected**, for reasons worth recording so it isn't revisited:
  - **It would deter exactly the people the window needs.** A rating multiplier cuts both ways, so at high rating the correct play becomes "don't battle during Sixes and Sevens, protect your rank" — the best roasters get a mathematical reason to sit out the hour whose whole purpose is to have them in it.
  - **It breaks what Elo is for.** K-factor is calibrated to how fast a skill estimate should converge; scaling it by time-of-day means two equally skilled players diverge based on their work schedule, contradicting this doc's "skill-based, not chance" premise. Someone who works evenings shouldn't be locked out of the fast lane.
  - **It would muddy the vote-confidence multiplier** (see functions/rating.js), which scales K by *evidence*. Stacking an arbitrary time-based multiplier on top of an evidence-based one makes K stop meaning anything.
  - **The desired effect happens anyway, honestly**: more people online during the window means more people voting, which means matches reach full vote confidence instead of being discounted. Playing during Sixes and Sevens genuinely does move rating more — not via a bonus, but because the result is actually being judged. Ungameable, and needs no new code.
  - **Related correction worth recording**: vote confidence is a *discount for thin evidence, capped at full* — never an amplifier. At the configured threshold and above you get the NORMAL rating change; below it you get a reduced one. Nobody ever gets more than standard K. Off-peak matches are therefore not "safer," just less-evidenced, and that discount disappears as volume grows.
  - **The 2x should apply to JUDGING during the window too, not just battling.** Votes are the scarce resource, and this closes the loop: more judges during Sixes and Sevens means more full-confidence results, which makes the window the place where rating actually moves.

## Ranking System
- **Rating — DECIDED**: Chess-style Elo-like numerical rating system, used as the underlying math (not shown directly to users — see Laugh Meter below). Everyone starts at a flat **1200**. No hard ceiling (unbounded). Soft floor at ~100 (prevents demoralizing bottomless losing spirals).
- **K-factor — DECIDED (variable, not flat)**: use a VARIABLE K-factor rather than one flat rate for all players — modeled on how real chess federations (USCF/FIDE) handle this. Lower/newer-tier players (roughly ranks 1-4) get a HIGH K-factor — bigger rating swings per match, faster early climbing, quick gratification, forgiving of an early loss or two. Higher-tier players (roughly Headliner and up, especially near the GOAT top-5 cutoff) get a LOW K-factor — smaller swings, rating only moves with sustained real performance, every win at the top genuinely has to be earned. Chosen over a hard "different system above X rank" split specifically because a single continuously-scaling K-factor achieves the same early-gratification-then-real-competition FEEL without a jarring rule-change moment a player could hit and feel blindsided by. Exact K-factor values per tier band not yet set — needs real tuning once analytics data exists (see Firebase Analytics decision).
- **Points/currency — DECIDED, separate system from rating**: a second currency that only ever increases (earned via participation, more for wins) — deliberately structured to never go down, unlike rating, so players on a losing streak still have something accumulating (retention/engagement reasoning). Spent on cosmetic-only unlocks (profile borders/frames, badge flair, alternate UI themes) — nothing that affects gameplay/matchmaking.
  - **Real-money purchase — DECIDED: earn-only for V1, revisit for V2**: recommendation was to eventually allow buying points via Apple/Google IAP (low legal risk since it's cosmetic-only, no prize/chance element unlike tournament entry fees) but NOT build this for V1 — validate that the cosmetic-unlock system actually drives engagement with earn-only points first, before investing in payment infrastructure for something unproven.
- **GOAT-tier exclusive perks — NEW, DECIDED (concept only)**: the top rank should unlock something beyond just the badge/title (special cosmetics, priority matchmaking, and/or access to exclusive GOAT-only tournaments) — ties back to original vision of "prestigious status, cool unlocks." Developer has not yet decided WHICH specific perks — still needs its own design pass/brainstorm.
- **Tier system — REVAMPED, DECIDED (single ladder, not nested leagues)**: originally considered separate "leagues" (Novice/Intermediate/Professional) each containing sub-ranks — simplified to ONE ordered ladder of 10 titles instead, since two coordinate systems (league + rank) is more confusing for users than one clear ladder. Each title implies its own tier — no separate league label needed.

    1. Average Joe
    2. Open Micer
    3. Class Clown
    4. The Funny Friend
    5. Door Guy
    6. Regular

  - **Bottom tier — DECIDED**: no 11th tier added below Average Joe. Average Joe remains the floor of the ladder.
  - **Promotion mechanic — DECIDED**: each tier requires BOTH a rating threshold AND a minimum number of ranked matches played to unlock (prevents a lucky early win streak from vaulting a low-sample-size account into a high tier — same logic as "placement matches" in games like League of Legends). Exact thresholds/match minimums not yet set — needs starting placement (1200) to land somewhere in the middle of the ladder, not at the bottom, so new players can move in either direction.
  - **GOAT tier — IMPORTANT EXCEPTION, DECIDED**: unlike ranks 1-9 (fixed rating thresholds), GOAT (rank 10) is capped at strictly the TOP 5 rated players platform-wide at any given time — a live leaderboard position, not a threshold. This is the ONE deliberate exception to the "fixed thresholds, not live percentiles" design principle used for every other rank — chosen specifically because exclusivity/scarcity fits GOAT's role as the single most prestigious tier (being bumped out by someone else's rise is part of what makes it meaningful, same as "#1 in the world" in real competitive systems). Practical implication: a GOAT-tier player can be demoted purely because a 6th player's rating surpassed theirs, even with no loss of their own — this should be reflected honestly in the rank-down popup messaging for this specific case if possible (a demotion here isn't necessarily "you got worse," it's "someone else got better"). Resolves the earlier "separate #1/Champion badge" question — the whole GOAT tier now IS that exclusive recognition, no separate badge needed.

Still show a partial progress indicator (e.g., the laugh meter visually filling toward next tier) even without exposing precise numbers. Note: GOAT's top-5 cutoff is inherently visible/comparative by nature (it's a leaderboard position), so "hidden criteria" mainly applies to ranks 1-9.
  - **Matchmaking — DECIDED**: pair users within the same tier first; if no match found, expand to ±1 tier. **Fallback — DECIDED**: if still no match, gradually widen the tier search range further over time (e.g., every 30 seconds expand the range) rather than waiting indefinitely or falling back to a truly unrestricted match — keeps matches reasonably skill-appropriate even during low-liquidity periods (early days, off-peak times) while still guaranteeing a match eventually.

Preserves skill differential while compressing early-farming advantage. Combined with the per-tier minimum-matches requirement, this naturally re-gates everyone through a mini placement period each season. **DECIDED**: each season should also come with exclusive seasonal cosmetics (limited-time items tied to that season, not permanently available) — proven retention pattern (Fortnite/League/Overwatch-style), pairs naturally with the reset as both a competitive fresh-start AND a content reason to return. ## Content Policy & Moderation
- **Free speech stance (explicit developer decision)**: offensive language, slurs, and no-holds-barred content are ALLOWED by design — this is a comedy platform, not a censored one. Users consent to this on signup.
- **NO real-time automated content moderation** (no auto-mute/auto-end on detected slurs) — explicitly rejected by developer to preserve free speech. This applies to CONTENT only.
- **Moderation model**: reactive only — human moderators review user-submitted reports after the fact. Bannable offense = intentional disruption/bullying NOT in service of comedy (admin discretion).
- **Technical quality flags (lighting/audio/camera) are separate from content moderation** — those are automated and do not touch what users say.
- **Visual content moderation (nudity/inappropriate physical actions) — DECIDED, separate from speech policy**: automated real-time detection for nudity/explicit physical acts during video, using an off-the-shelf visual moderation API (e.g., AWS Rekognition, Google Cloud Vision SafeSearch, Azure Content Moderator, or specialized providers like Hive/Sightengine). On detection: auto-blur video feed and/or auto-end the match. This is VISUAL/ACTION moderation only — it does not touch audio, speech, or language, and does not conflict with the free-speech content policy above. Treat as its own module (e.g., `visual_moderation_service.dart`) wrapped behind an interface like the other external dependencies, so the provider can be swapped later.
- **Build Order step 9a implementation status**: provider chosen is **Google Cloud Vision SafeSearch** — picked over AWS Rekognition/Azure/Hive/Sightengine specifically because it lives in the same GCP project as everything else (no new vendor account, shares billing/auth with Firebase), which matters given the "solo dev, no backend experience, low/no budget" constraints stated throughout this doc. `VisualModerationService` (`lib/core/services/visual_moderation_service.dart`) + `CloudVisionModerationService` implementation + `moderatePhoto` Cloud Function (`functions/visualModeration.js`) are built, deployed, and verified live: a real uploaded image was correctly approved end-to-end through the app (upload to Storage → moderate → save to `profile.photoUrls` → UI updates), and removal was verified too. Rejects at SafeSearch's LIKELY/VERY_LIKELY bands for adult, racy, or violent content.
  - **This covers PROFILE PHOTOS.** Live in-match video moderation is now ALSO built, tested live on a real two-device match, and working: `AgoraVideoCallService` registers a `VideoFrameObserver` before `joinChannel` (confirmed live this DOES work - the earlier "unimplemented stub" claim was a misdiagnosis, see Build Order step 3's corrected status note) and exposes a throttled `remoteFrameSamples` stream (one sample roughly every 4 seconds per device, via `onRenderVideoFrame`) through the `VideoCallService` interface as a provider-agnostic `RawVideoFrame`. `MatchScreen` converts each sample from raw I420/YUV420 to JPEG off the UI thread (`lib/core/services/yuv_to_jpeg.dart`, run via `compute()`), then calls the new `checkImageBytes` method on `VisualModerationService` (implemented by `CloudVisionModerationService`, hitting the new `moderateMatchFrame` Cloud Function - SafeSearch on inline base64 bytes, no Storage upload, since a passing frame has no reason to be kept anywhere). **Response on detection, per an explicit developer decision**: auto-end the match immediately (not just auto-blur, which the frame-observer API can't do - see below) AND auto-file a report into the SAME `reports` collection/admin-review pipeline used for human-submitted reports (Build Order step 9) - this is deliberately NOT an automatic ban/suspend, consistent with the case-by-case admin-discretion moderation model elsewhere in this doc. Both participants see an explanatory end-of-match screen (different copy for the reporting device vs. the other participant, who's notified via the existing Agora data-stream messaging).
    - **A real race-condition bug was found and fixed during live testing**: frame sampling must not start until the opponent's Firebase uid is known (exchanged asynchronously via the existing `'identity'` data-stream message), because `_handleContentViolation`'s report-filing is guarded on having a `reportedUserId` to file against. The original code started sampling right after `joinChannel()` succeeded, which could race ahead of the identity exchange - proven live with a deliberately-instant forced-rejection test double, which silently produced NO Firestore report despite the UI looking correct (the failure was swallowed into a `debugPrint`, not surfaced to the user). Fixed by moving the `remoteFrameSamples` subscription into the `'identity'` message handler; re-verified live afterward with a full two-device run confirming the report now persists correctly, and that both the reporting device's and the other participant's end-of-match UI render correctly.

### Intellectual Property — patents vs. trademark — NEW, ASSESSED
**Question raised by the developer**: should patents be pursued for a roasting app?

**Recommendation: no patents. Do the trademark instead.** Reasoning, recorded so it isn't re-opened without new information:
- **Software/business-method patents are largely unavailable for this.** Since *Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank* (2014), abstract ideas implemented on a computer are generally unpatentable. "Randomly pair two users for a video comedy contest judged by community vote" is a strong candidate for rejection under §101 — matching people, running a contest and tallying votes are all things done long before computers.
- **The prior art is dense.** Chatroulette and Omegle (random stranger video pairing, 2009-2010), rap-battle and karaoke-battle apps, vote-judged competition formats on Twitch, the "Roast Me" subreddit, and televised roast-battle formats all predate this. The specific combination is fresh; the individual pieces are not.
- **Cost is disproportionate.** A US utility patent runs roughly $10-20k through an attorney over 2-4 years — a large fraction of this project's stated low/no budget, spent on something unlikely to issue and narrow if it did.
- **Enforcement is the real barrier, and it's decisive.** Patent litigation costs six to seven figures. A solo developer cannot realistically enforce one against a funded copycat, so even a granted patent would mostly be decorative. Patents protect parties who can afford to sue.
- **A provisional patent** (~$130-320 at micro/small entity rates, 12 months of "patent pending") is the cheap middle option, but for a business-method app it's mostly investor optics and it starts a 12-month clock that forces the expensive decision anyway. Skip unless fundraising makes the label worth something.

**What actually protects this business, in priority order:**
1. **Trademark on "The Bully League"** — cheaper (~$250-350 per class in government fees, $1-2k with an attorney), far more enforceable, and it protects the thing that actually accrues value: the name people search for. This upgrades the existing unresolved trademark-clearance item from "eventually" to "the main IP action worth taking," ideally **before** a public launch makes the name valuable and before someone else files.
   - **Specific reason for urgency, connecting to an existing finding**: the Branding section records that `@thebullyleague` on Instagram is **already taken by a real, unrelated account calling itself "TBL Official."** If that account represents a business genuinely using the name in commerce, it could establish common-law trademark rights that conflict with this project's — which is exactly the kind of thing a clearance search exists to surface. That's a concrete, known reason to do the search rather than a generic precaution.
2. **Copyright** — automatic on the code, brand assets and produced clips, no filing required (registration only matters for statutory damages if suing).
3. **The actual moat: network effects, the accumulated content library, and the audience.** A competitor can clone the mechanic in a month; they cannot clone the users, the match archive, or the TikTok following. Speed and brand are the defensible assets here, not legal exclusivity over the idea.

**Defensive angle** (could someone else's patent block this?): practically low risk — random video chat has existed for 15+ years without patent trolls shutting the category down. If the app ever grows enough to be a target, that's a good problem and it will come with the budget for real counsel.

**Caveat worth noting**: if something genuinely novel and non-obvious gets invented on the *technical* side, that's more patentable than the product concept. Nothing built so far qualifies — the audio/video timeline alignment, for instance, uses timestamps Agora already documents, which is competent engineering rather than invention.

## Compliance / Account Management — NEW NOTE
- **CCPA data deletion — BUILT.** `functions/accountDeletion.js` + the `deleteMyAccount` callable + `DeleteAccountScreen`, reachable from the profile screen. Original requirement: since developer is California-based with California users, need a user-facing "delete my account / delete my data" flow in account settings from V1 — required under CCPA, cheaper to build in from the start than retrofit once support requests start coming in.
  - **The hard part is that a match has TWO people in it.** One person asking to be erased must not destroy the other's footage or rating history. So everything genuinely *theirs* is deleted, while shared records survive but no longer point at a person.
  - **Recording each player separately turns out to matter here.** Because raw footage is per-player files rather than one baked composite, a deleting user's own video is removed while their opponent's survives. Under the previous composite recording that would have been impossible — the only options would have been destroying the opponent's footage too, or keeping video of someone who asked to be erased. That wasn't a consideration when individual mode was chosen; it's a second payoff.
  - **Deleted**: the user document (profile, ammo text, hometown, interests, push tokens), profile photos, their own raw match footage, unpublished rendered highlights (a composite necessarily contains them), any matchmaking queue entry, and the Firebase Auth account itself.
  - **Deliberately kept**: highlights already published (CLAUDE.md's existing position — consented under the ToS at the time, already publicly distributed); match documents and results, because the opponent's rating history depends on them and the website feed already renders an unresolvable player as "Unknown"; ballots they cast, since deleting those would retroactively change other people's outcomes; and moderation reports, because otherwise someone could erase their own history by deleting and re-registering, which is exactly the abuse the report system exists to catch.
  - **Ordering matters**: queue entries go first so a half-finished deletion can never leave someone pairable, and the auth account goes last so an earlier failure leaves an account that can still sign in and retry rather than an orphaned identity.
  - A `deletedAccounts/{uid}` record is written as compliance evidence. It holds no personal data — once the user document and auth account are gone, the uid maps to nobody.
  - **The screen states plainly what survives**, which is both the honest thing and the safer one: a user who discovered the retained published clips afterwards would rightly feel misled, and an app store reviewer asking "what does delete actually delete?" gets the answer on the screen. Guarded by typing DELETE plus a confirmation dialog.
  - **Verified end-to-end against the deployed backend**: a throwaway account paired with a surviving opponent, played a match, then deleted — its document is gone, it can no longer sign in, and the opponent's account, the shared match record and its result all survive intact. 10 local tests pin the policy, including that only an explicit `published === true` spares a highlight (anything ambiguous falls to deletion, since keeping someone's video after they asked to be erased is the worse mistake).
  - **SUBCOLLECTIONS ARE NOW PURGED TOO — a real compliance bug, found and fixed.** Deleting a Firestore document does **not** touch its subcollections: they survive as orphans, reachable by anyone who knows the uid and invisible in the console because the parent is gone. This flow was written and verified before `pointsLedger` and `ratingHistory` existed, so by the time both did, a CCPA deletion was leaving behind what a user earned and spent with dates, and every rated match with its date and their opponent id. Now uses `recursiveDelete` on the user document.
    - **Recursion deliberately, rather than naming the two known subcollections.** A hardcoded list is exactly what went stale here, and would go stale again the next time one is added. The `deletedAccounts` record now carries `subcollectionsPurged`, so the sweep having run is evidenced rather than assumed.
    - **Falls back to deleting the document alone** if the recursive sweep fails — leaving the whole profile in place because a subcollection sweep errored would be far worse.
    - **Verified against the deployed backend**: an account with real entries in both subcollections is deleted, both come back empty, the compliance record confirms the sweep ran, and the opponent survives untouched. 7 live checks.
    - **The general lesson worth carrying: adding a subcollection under `users/{uid}` silently creates a deletion gap.** Anything added there in future has to be checked against this flow.
  - **Known gaps**: no re-authentication is required before deletion, so a stolen session could trigger it — the typed confirmation is friction, not security. There is also no grace period or undo; deletion is immediate and irreversible.

## Clip Takedown & Participant Objection — DECIDED (not yet built)
Raised by the developer: what protects someone who is unhappy that their match footage is public — because they were offended, feel defamed, or are receiving hate. Audit found the **takedown machinery exists but no user-facing path to it does**: `publishHighlight` genuinely revokes access (clearing the Storage token makes every issued URL fail), but report categories are all about the opponent's *conduct*, support categories are billing/bug/account, nothing checks for an objection before publishing, and **nobody is told when their clip goes public** — so they cannot object to something they don't know exists.

**TWO CHANNELS, deliberately behaving differently.** This split is the core of the design and everything else follows from it:
- **"I'd rather this wasn't posted"** — preference. Deadline applies, cap applies, friction is acceptable.
- **"This is harming me"** — harassment, brigading, doxxing, a factual claim dressed as a joke. **No deadline, no cap, no friction, ever.** Harm surfaces weeks later precisely *because* a clip spread. Apple's Guideline 1.2 requires an accessible flagging mechanism and GDPR consent-withdrawal cannot be gated, so obstructing this channel is both a compliance problem and the wrong thing.

**DECIDED:**
- **Takedown of your own footage is UNCONDITIONAL**, not at admin discretion. It costs one clip, and it is a strong trust signal for an app whose premise is letting a stranger humiliate you on camera. It is also a far better answer to a store reviewer than "case by case."
- **The opt-out deadline is when the voting period ends.** The developer's own idea and a good one: deciding *before* the result is known means the choice is made on "am I comfortable with this being public" rather than "I lost, delete it." Needs no new clock — voting already closes 24h after completion — and nobody can time it against a result.
- **Cap: 2 preference-based requests per month**, published openly like the skip cap rather than hidden friction. **FLAGGED BY THE DEVELOPER TO REVISIT AS MONETIZATION** — extra takedowns as a subscription perk or purchasable with points.
  - **If that is built, the guardrails are not optional.** It is a *different shape* from the pay-to-remove model already rejected (see below), and only stays different if: (1) **the harm channel is always free and uncapped** — someone being harassed must never meet a paywall; (2) the cap and any monetization apply **only** to preference requests; (3) it is never framed as "pay to remove," because selling extra convenience is defensible and selling relief from distress is not; (4) **GDPR erasure/consent-withdrawal can never be paywalled**, so a UK user's removal request is always free regardless of cap.
- **Anti-abuse of the harm channel — honour first, enforce after.** The developer asked for abuse protection here, and the honest answer is that it cannot be pre-filtered without breaking the channel. So: no gate at the point of request; require a specific harm reason (harassment / doxxing / false factual claim / brigading), which is *routing* rather than friction and makes bad-faith use visible; log every request against the account; and treat demonstrated repeated bad-faith use as its own actionable behaviour under the existing moderation model. This is how mature platforms handle urgent takedowns.

**BUILT AND VERIFIED.** Enforcement lives in publishHighlight itself rather than in a note an admin might miss: a participant objection blocks publication outright, and publication is refused while the objection window is still open, so nothing reaches a public audience before both players have had their full chance to opt out. An existing publication is REVOKED immediately on request - a takedown that only stopped future posting would do nothing for the person whose clip is already out, which is exactly when someone asks. Both players are now notified when a clip goes public, without which every safeguard here is theoretical: you cannot object to something you do not know exists. The client flow is ClipTakedownSheet, reachable from My Battles, and its most important line is that removing a clip does NOT change the result - most delete requests are really attempts to erase a loss, and saying the loss stands removes the motive without obstructing anyone. Harm requests route into the existing reports queue AFTER the takedown has already happened. Verified with 17 local tests and 7 against the deployed backend. KNOWN GAP, accepted by the developer rather than built: a clip published after voting closed cannot be opted out of by preference, only reported as harm - one config change if that is revisited.

**Related decision, already settled: NO CHARGING FOR TAKEDOWN.** The developer asked whether removal could be charged for, given posting costs money. Answer was no, and the reasoning is recorded so it is not revisited: it matches the mugshot-removal business model that drew FTC/state-AG enforcement and extortion charges; it destroys the consent defence by documenting that the person objects and the clip stayed up for money; **GDPR Article 7(3) requires withdrawing consent to be as easy as giving it**, so it is a straight violation for the UK launch; app stores would treat it as an abusive practice; and the headline writes itself. The economics also do not justify it — a render costs roughly $0.10. **The real fix is ordering, not billing**: collect the objection *before* rendering, so the money is never spent on footage that cannot be used.

## Video Retention Policy — DECIDED, and now ENFORCED
- **BUILT**: `purgeExpiredRecordings` (`functions/recordingRetention.js`, scheduled daily) deletes raw footage for matches older than 7 days, keeping anything marked `recording.published`. Only an explicit `published === true` counts as published; anything ambiguous falls to purge.

Since brackets are async/rolling (not scheduled live events), there's no real scheduling conflict forcing a one-at-a-time rule — a user can play multiple async tournament matches on their own time. Restricting would just cost entry-fee revenue with no real benefit.
- **Bracket seeding — DECIDED**: random seeding (chosen as most fair over rating-based seeding). - **Tournament withdrawal — DECIDED**: a user CAN withdraw from a tournament and get refunded, as long as it's before the bracket actually starts. Separate from the "tournament cancelled due to insufficient entrants" refund rule already decided — this is a voluntary individual withdrawal, not a tournament-wide cancellation.

  - **Real bug found and fixed**: the first bracket-generation approach shuffled real entrants and null "bye" placeholders together into one flat list before pairing sequentially. That can randomly land two bye placeholders in the same matchup (both players null) — a matchup with no one to advance, silently breaking that entire tournament. Caught by a 2,400-trial local simulation (200 trials each across 12 entrant counts, asserting a valid single winner every time) before ever touching a device — the naive version failed 4 of 12 sizes (5, 9, 17, 1 entrants). Fixed by peeling the correct number of byes off the shuffled entrant list FIRST (which is always possible byeCount is always less than half the bracket size by construction), giving each one its own matchup, then pairing the remainder — guarantees at most one bye per matchup regardless of entrant count. Confirmed live on an emulator with a real 3-entrant tournament (1 bye) advancing correctly to a real winner, not just in the local simulation.
  - **REAL RESULTS NOW DRIVE THE BRACKET — BUILT.** `applyResultToBracket` + `recordTournamentResult` (`functions/tournament.js`), called from `finalizeMatch` whenever a `mode: "tournament"` match settles. A match carries its slot as `tournament: {tournamentId, roundNumber, matchupIndex}`, its real `winnerId` fills that matchup, and the round advances the moment every matchup in it is settled — completing the tournament when one winner remains. **This replaces `Math.random()`**, which was the single most obviously-wrong thing in the codebase: a bracket decided by coin flip is a simulator, not a competition, and prizes will eventually ride on it. `debugAdvanceRound` stays for testing the mechanics.
    - **The winner must be one of the two players in that matchup**, or the result is refused. A mis-stamped match could otherwise advance somebody who was never in the tournament, and a bracket is exactly where that must not happen.
    - **Idempotent**, because finalization is retryable: a settled matchup is left alone, so a repeat neither overwrites a result nor appends a duplicate round. A test covers re-applying the *final* result specifically, since that is the case that would have added a phantom round.
    - **A late result for an EARLIER round fills its gap without rebuilding rounds already under way**, and the pure function never mutates its argument — the caller decides what to persist.
    - **Best-effort**: a bracket that fails to advance is recoverable by an admin, whereas throwing would fail a finalization that had already applied real rating changes.
    - **Verified against the deployed backend** with a real 4-entrant bracket driven entirely by real ballots: a match settles its matchup, the round holds while another is open, it advances and pairs the two real winners, the final completes the tournament with the right winner, and no phantom round is appended. **9 live checks plus 12 local tests.**
  - **PLAYING A TOURNAMENT MATCH — BUILT** (`functions/tournamentPlay.js`, `startTournamentMatch`). This was the missing half: ordinary matchmaking pairs two people out of a queue, but a bracket matchup is two **named** players who must face each other, so none of the queue machinery applies.
    - **The match id is DERIVED from the bracket slot** (`t_{tournamentId}_r{round}_m{index}`) rather than generated. Two players tapping start at the same instant would otherwise create two documents for one matchup and each sit alone in a different channel waiting for someone who is never coming. A deterministic id plus a transaction makes that impossible by construction.
    - Returns the **same pairing shape** the matchmaking flow hands back, so the client reuses the whole existing match path rather than growing a second one. Bracket order decides player1/player2, so both clients agree on the fixed Agora uids without negotiating.
    - **Arrival is recorded** (`arrivedAt`) the moment someone starts, because that — not completion — is what forfeits are judged on. Somebody who turned up and whose opponent never did must not be punished for the match being unplayable.
  - **ASYNC ROUND WINDOWS AND FORFEITS — BUILT.** Every round now carries `windowStartMs`/`windowEndMs` (24h default, per-tournament overridable, bounds-checked), set when round 1 is generated and when each later round is built. `tournamentForfeits` sweeps every 30 minutes.
    - **Without the sweep the window would be decoration**, and an async bracket stalls the first time one entrant loses interest, holding up everyone behind them.
    - **CLAUDE.md's rule, implemented**: a no-show is an auto-loss and the opponent advances — otherwise entering and never playing would be a risk-free way to get an entry fee back. If **both** miss it, both are eliminated and **nobody** advances, rather than handing a free pass to someone who also failed to turn up.
    - A forfeit is routed through the **same `applyResultToBracket`** a played result uses, so the two advance a bracket identically.
    - **Emptied slots propagate correctly**: whoever faces one advances unopposed like a round-1 bye, and two empty slots meeting are marked `isDead` — an unsettleable matchup would otherwise block its round from ever advancing. A final round where everyone no-showed cancels the tournament rather than leaving it running with no possible winner.
    - **The sweep is guarded against a malformed bracket**, found when a test corrupted one: brackets are hand-editable in the Firebase console (the documented admin tool), and one bad tournament must not stop the sweep settling the others.
    - **Worth remembering: Firestore cannot address array elements by dotted field path.** `bracket.rounds.1.windowEndMs` does not update the second round — it writes a nested map and corrupts the array. Read-modify-write the whole array instead.
    - **Verified against the deployed backend** with four real accounts: a player starts their matchup, the opponent lands in **the same** match with a complementary Agora uid and can see the first arrived, the match links back for the result pipeline, an outsider is refused, a no-show forfeits and the survivor advances into a real final with its own fresh window, and a both-no-show final cancels the tournament. **13 live checks plus 17 local tests.**
  - **CLIENT UI — BUILT and verified on a device.** `TournamentDetailScreen` gained a **"Play your match"** button, shown only when this player genuinely has a matchup to play — never for a bye, someone already knocked out, or a closed round, since the server refuses all three and offering an action certain to fail reads as a bug. Those cases get a plain statement instead ("You have a bye", "You are out of this tournament").
    - **Routed through recording consent and the camera check**, exactly like an ordinary match. Consent is not optional — a tournament match is recorded and highlight-eligible — and the camera check matters *more* here, since a bracket match cannot simply be requeued if the setup is bad. `PreMatchScreen` gained an optional `tournamentId` and sends the player to the lobby instead of the queue.
    - **`TournamentLobbyScreen` replaces the bio reveal for tournaments, deliberately.** The bio reveal assumes both players are present and counts down; a tournament round is **async by design**, so the honest state is "you are checked in, they have not arrived yet" with no countdown pretending otherwise. It offers **no skip and no requeue** — you cannot decline your bracket opponent and there is no queue to return to. Both sides watch the match document, so whoever arrives second starts the battle for both.
    - **It says plainly that leaving is safe** ("You can leave and come back before the round closes. If they never turn up, the win is yours"), because otherwise checking out feels like forfeiting when checking IN is exactly what protects you.
    - **Verified on a device end to end**: the button appears for an entrant, consent and the camera check run, and the lobby shows "vs SeventhVoter / You are checked in / Round closes in about 5h". Probe tournament deleted afterwards.
    - **Small finding while testing**: the tournament list orders by `createdAt`, so a hand-seeded tournament without that field is invisible in the app while existing perfectly well in Firestore. Worth knowing when creating tournaments by hand in the console, which is the documented admin path.
  - **TOURNAMENT NOTIFICATIONS — BUILT** (`functions/tournamentNotify.js`, `tournamentNotifications` scheduled every 30 minutes). Two per round: **your round is open**, and a **warning two hours before it closes**.
    - **This earns a second notification where vote reminders get one a day.** Every other notification in the app is an invitation - miss it and you missed a battle. Missing an async round window is a **forfeit**: out of a tournament you paid to enter, without ever playing. A round that opens silently is a trap.
    - **The closing warning goes ONLY to people who have not checked in**, read from the same `arrivedAt` signal the forfeit sweep judges on. Someone who already turned up is in no danger, and chasing them is pure noise.
    - **A late recovery warns rather than announcing an opening.** If the job is down through the start of a round and recovers inside the last two hours, "your round is open" is technically true and practically useless - what they need is "play now or forfeit". The copy says the consequence out loud for the same reason.
    - **Byes and decided matchups are skipped**, and the marker lives on the round itself, claimed BEFORE sending - missing one announcement costs a round, repeating it is how an app earns an OS-level mute.
    - **Verified by running the real sweep against real Firestore**: an open round is announced to both players and marked on the bracket, a second sweep says nothing, near the deadline only the absentee is warned, nobody is chased once both have checked in, and closed or decided rounds send nothing. **7 live checks plus 15 local tests.**

  - Points reward per vote cast (using existing points currency) — naturally self-limiting since it's one vote per account per match, so no farming risk from voting itself.
  - Daily voting streak bonus for consecutive days with at least one vote cast.

Titles/thresholds not yet designed.
  - **NON-POINTS REWARDS FOR VOTING — NEW, DECIDED (developer request): judging other people's matches must earn a player something USEFUL, not only cosmetic currency.** Rationale: points buy profile borders and badge flair, which is a weak motivator for exactly the competitive players whose votes matter most — they care about the ladder, not about frames. A utility reward makes voting worth doing for someone who will never spend a point. Concrete candidates, not yet chosen or designed:
    - **Extra skips** (the developer's own suggestion, and the most directly valuable). **CRITICAL CONSTRAINT if this is built**: skips exist to be scarce — the 3/day cap is what stops opponent cherry-picking, which corrupts ranked and is a collusion-avoidance vector (see Multi-Account / Collusion Prevention). If voting can mint skips without a ceiling, a determined user votes ten times and gets effectively unlimited skips, reintroducing precisely the problem the cap prevents. Worse, **skips are worth MORE to a rating-manipulator than to an honest player** — someone farming rating specifically wants to dodge strong opponents. So earned skips need a hard total daily ceiling (e.g. never more than 5/day however many votes are cast), and they must expire with the daily reset rather than banking up.
    - **Priority matchmaking** — paired first, or ahead of the queue, especially during Sixes and Sevens. Probably the best option of the three: it has essentially no abuse surface, and it rewards the people who keep the ecosystem alive with faster access to the thing they want. It also directly helps the liquidity problem rather than merely not hurting it.
    - **The "Judge" progression track** below is the natural home for all of this — the reward for judging should ladder, not just accumulate.
    - **Explicitly flagged as dangerous, do NOT do casually: increasing a frequent voter's VOTE WEIGHT.** It's the obvious-sounding reward and it's the wrong one — it concentrates influence over match outcomes in a small group and is straightforwardly gameable. This doc already files reputation-weighted voting under V2 vote-integrity work for the same reason; it should not sneak in through the incentives door.
    - **Tournament entries as a voting reward** are tempting but carry a real economic cost once entry fees are live (see Prize & Tournament Legal Structure) — minting them via voting means giving away something with cash value. Fine while entries are points-only; revisit before cash activates.
  - Exact point values and streak bonus amounts still need real playtesting to balance — structure decided, numbers not yet set.
  - **Daily/weekly quest system — NEW, DECIDED**: add a lightweight quest system layered on top of streaks (e.g. "vote on 3 matches today," "win 1 match this week") tied to the existing points currency as rewards. Rationale: research on 2026 app retention shows apps combining streaks + quests + progression ("meaningful play," not just raw points) see meaningfully higher Day-30 retention than points-only systems — gives users explicit direction on what to do each session rather than just a vague reason to open the app. Quest content/rotation/exact rewards not yet designed.
- **Voting guardrails — DESIGNED AND LARGELY BUILT** (was: flagged by the developer for a dedicated design conversation; that conversation happened). What shipped:
  - **Confidence-weighted rating, chosen over a minimum-vote threshold.** `voteConfidence` in `functions/rating.js` scales the K-factor by total vote weight, capped at 1.0, so a thinly-judged match moves rating proportionally less while a well-judged one moves the full normal amount. **Nobody ever gets MORE than standard K — it is a discount for thin evidence, never an amplifier.** The threshold is `fullConfidenceVotes` in `config/matchSettings` (default 10), so it is live-configurable as volume grows, and is read from the settings **stamped on the match at pairing time** — a match is judged by the rules in force when it was played, not by whatever the config says a day later. `finalizeMatch` records the resulting `voteConfidence` on the match document so a thin result is explicable after the fact.
    - **Why not a minimum-vote threshold**, which was the obvious answer: a threshold creates a cliff — below it nothing happens, above it the full swing — so there's an incentive to farm exactly enough votes and no reason to care beyond that. Worse, at private-beta volume most matches would close *under* the threshold, nobody's rating would move, and the prestige loop the whole ranking system exists to drive would never start. That's protecting the ladder by switching it off.
    - **Concrete effect on the collusion problem this was opened for**: at the time of writing a single friendly vote moved 14 points at 1200 rating, and 29 such wins reached the Headliner threshold. Under this curve the same climb needs roughly ten times as many matches. 11 local tests in `functions/test/rating.test.js` pin exactly this, including that one vote moves 1–3 points and that full confidence is a no-op.
  - **Cold-start liquidity — the needs-votes queue.** `getMatchesNeedingVotes` (`functions/voteQueue.js`) + `VoteQueueScreen` surface open matches **fewest votes first**, not most recent. A recency feed sends everyone to the same newest match while older ones close unjudged; ordering by need means one ballot on a zero-vote match does far more for the ladder than the eleventh on a popular one. It runs server-side because ballots are deliberately not client-readable. The ask is made at the **end of a match** — the highest-intent moment in the app, where the framing is plainly reciprocal ("judge a few while yours is being judged") rather than extractive — and from Home as "Judge a Battle".
  - **Live scoreboard — BUILT, and gated.** `onVoteCast` (`functions/voteCount.js`) now writes `matches/{matchId}/tally/live` with per-player counts alongside the existing raw `voteCount`. `LiveTally` (`lib/widgets/live_tally.dart`) renders it as a live Firestore listener, used on the vote screen and on a new `MyBattlesScreen` ("Watch the votes come in" from the verdict screen, "My Battles" from Home). This is the frantic-refresh experience the developer asked for, and it matters most around tournaments where a bracket place rides on the count.
    - **The tally is HIDDEN until you vote — per an explicit developer decision** ("full live scoreboard but don't show anyone the tally until after they vote"). Seeing who is ahead before judging biases the judgement, and it would tell someone rallying support exactly how many more ballots they need. **Participants are the deliberate exception**: they cannot vote on their own match at all, so there is no judgement of theirs left to bias, and watching your own result arrive is the entire point of the feature.
    - **It lives in a subcollection specifically so it can be gated differently from the match document**, which must stay readable by everyone for the feed.
    - **This forced a related tightening: ballot reads were locked down to your own ballot only.** `votes/{matchId}/ballots/{voterId}` was previously readable by any signed-in user so the client could sum the tally itself — which would have made the new gate pure decoration, since any client could add the ballots up and see the score anyway. Ballots also name who voted for whom, which is the raw material for coordinating a brigade. Reading your own is still allowed because the client legitimately needs to know whether you have already judged a match.
    - **Verified against the deployed backend** with three real accounts: the trigger writes the tally and credits the right player, a signed-in non-voter is refused (403), a user who has voted can read it, a participant can read their own match, and one user cannot read another's ballot. 11 checks, plus 6 local tests on the pure tally-attribution helper.
  - **RESOLVED (this note was stale).** The 24-hour vote window is measured from `completedAt`, with `createdAt` only as a fallback for a document that somehow lacks one - see `voteWindowStartMs` in `functions/matchFinalization.js`, which is the single source the client countdown, `castVote` and `finalizeMatch` all read. A match that sat pending no longer loses part of its voting window.
  - **Can't vote on yourself — ALREADY BUILT.** Two distinct server-side checks in `castVote`, easy to conflate:
    - **Who may vote**: any signed-in account EXCEPT the match's two participants ("Match participants can't vote on their own match"). This is the only restriction on who can cast a ballot — voting is deliberately open to the whole userbase, which is the entire point of the Spectator tier.
    - **Who may be voted FOR**: the `votedForPlayerId` must be one of that match's two players. This blocks a ballot naming a third party or a fabricated uid; it says nothing about who is allowed to vote.
    Kept in this list only so it isn't re-litigated as missing.
  - **Cold-start voting liquidity — PARTLY ADDRESSED, still worth watching.** Incentives assume there are users to incentivise; with very few accounts a match can close its window with near-zero ballots. The needs-votes queue and the post-match reciprocal ask above are the built response, and confidence weighting means a thin result now quietly counts for less rather than settling a rating change on one friend's vote. Still available if it isn't enough: seeding votes from the beta group as an explicit early-phase measure, or extending the window while the pool is small. **Sixes and Sevens helps here too** — concentrating players in time concentrates judges in time.
  - **Why these had to be designed together**: a minimum-vote threshold is only safe *if* liquidity is solved, otherwise it stalls the ladder rather than protecting it. Scaling by confidence sidesteps that dependency entirely, which is the main reason it won over a threshold.
- **Vote integrity — advanced layer (V2+)**: device/IP fingerprinting to catch multiple accounts voting from the same device (needs to be signal-weighted, not a hard block, since legitimate device/network sharing happens), reputation-weighted voting (established accounts' votes count more than brand-new ones), and anomaly detection for suspicious vote spikes routed to manual review. These are beyond V1 scope/infrastructure.

## In-App Clip Playback — NEW, BUILT (and a gap it exposes)
- **Until this, the app could not play video at all.** No player dependency existed, `VoteScreen` had no player, and `getMatchesNeedingVotes` didn't even return a clip URL — so in-app judging meant reading two usernames and picking one. That makes a vote noise rather than judgement, and it quietly undermines the vote-confidence weighting, which assumes more votes means a better-judged match. The website played clips; the app did not.
- `MatchClipPlayer` (`lib/widgets/match_clip_player.dart`, using `video_player`) now sits at the TOP of the vote screen, above the winner choice. It prefers the **vertical** rendition (this is a phone; the landscape cut is a letterboxed strip), loops (judges often want a second look at a line), and uses tap-to-pause with no chrome over the faces.
- **The dependency was de-risked before any code was written**, following the pattern that worked for `firebase_messaging`: added it and ran a real build first, given this project's documented AGP/KGP fragility. `video_player` builds clean against the pinned AGP 8.9.1 / KGP 2.1.0.
- **IMPORTANT GAP THIS EXPOSED, not yet resolved**: the player renders an honest "clip not available yet" for most matches, because **rendering is on-demand and admin-only, and publishing is a deliberate human gate**. So in-app judging is still mostly blind in practice. Closing it needs two decisions that are really one conversation:
  1. **Auto-render every ranked match** rather than on demand (roughly $0.10 each, dominated by Speech-to-Text).
  2. **Serve unreviewed renders to voters in-app.** `storage.rules` currently denies clients all read on `match_highlights/**` precisely because a render is unreviewed. The natural resolution is the **in-app vs external split** already proposed for takedowns: in-app visibility is part of playing a ranked match and is what makes judging possible, while external posting keeps the marketing-grade human gate. Mitigations for unreviewed in-app clips are the standard UGC ones already in place — signed-in 18+ users who consented to mature content, a report button on the same screen, and the Apple 1.2 24-hour action commitment.
- **PLAYBACK IN THE JUDGE FEED — RESOLVED, verified on a device.** `getWatchFeed` hands the client a URL two different ways, and both now work:
  - **A PUBLISHED clip uses its permanent public URL.** Publishing already mints one via a Firebase Storage download token, so the feed prefers `highlight.publicUrls` when `published === true` — no signing call at all.
  - **An UNPUBLISHED render gets a 6-hour SIGNED URL**, precisely because it has *not* passed human review and must not become permanently public. The two paths are complementary rather than redundant; keep both.
  - **Signing was broken at first, and the diagnosis is the part worth keeping.** Every clip came back null with `Permission 'iam.serviceAccounts.signBlob' denied`. Cloud Functions run as the compute service account (`283010814552-compute@developer.gserviceaccount.com`), which has **no private key of its own** and must call the IAM signBlob API to sign anything — a permission it lacks by default. Fixed by a one-time console grant of **`Service Account Token Creator`** to that account (IAM & Admin → IAM; tick "Include Google-provided role grants" or the account is hidden). Same class of one-time console step as enabling Cloud Vision, Speech-to-Text, Storage and the Realtime Database before it.
  - **The fix genuinely could not be automated — verified by attempt.** The Admin SDK service account was used to try reading and then setting the project IAM policy; both were refused with "The caller does not have permission". A service account cannot grant itself IAM roles, by design.
  - **A genuinely misleading class of bug worth remembering**: signing was probed *successfully* from this machine before deploying, using the **website's Admin SDK service-account key** — which holds a private key and therefore signs **locally**, never touching IAM. Local success says nothing about whether a deployed function can sign. Verify signing from inside the function.
  - **IAM grants do not take effect instantly.** The first check after the grant still failed, which looked like the wrong principal had been granted; it simply had not propagated. When a permission fix appears not to have worked, re-check a minute later before re-diagnosing. The runtime identity was confirmed the reliable way — a temporary log of the metadata server's `service-accounts/default/email` from inside the function, since Cloud Run's config is not readable with the credentials available here.

## Spectator Accounts — NEW, DECIDED
- **Two-tier account system**: split account creation into a lightweight **Spectator** tier and the full **Battler** tier.
  - **Spectator account**: lightweight signup (phone/email verification only). Can browse the in-app discovery feed, watch matches, and vote. Skips photos, bio/profile fields, tutorial, and manual approval entirely — those are only required for the Battler tier.
  - **Battler profile**: the full existing flow (5 photos, bio fields, tutorial, manual approval) — only required once/if a Spectator decides they want to queue for an actual match. A user can upgrade from Spectator to Battler at any time.
  - Rationale: forcing every download through the full battler onboarding (photos + manual approval + tutorial) creates unnecessary friction for someone who just wants to watch/vote after seeing a shared clip — costly for a growth-focused launch. Spectators also become a natural, low-friction pool of voters for the voting-incentive idea above.
- **Voting integrity for Spectators — DECIDED (V1 baseline)**: since spectator accounts skip manual approval, voting-fraud defenses lean more on automated signals:
  1. Phone verification required (already standard for all accounts) — block VOIP/virtual numbers specifically at signup (most phone verification providers, including Firebase's, can detect and reject these) to prevent cheap mass burner-account creation.
  2. CAPTCHA at the moment of voting (already decided elsewhere).
  3. One vote per account per match (already implicit in data model).

## Async Matchmaking Revision — NEW, DECIDED (not yet built)
**The problem, raised by the developer**: with a small user base spread across timezones, live 1v1 random pairing is close to impossible — two people have to be awake AND in the app in the same minute. This is the single biggest threat to the private beta, since the core loop simply won't fire.

**Developer's original proposal**: pressing matchmaking pairs you with whoever queued most recently (even hours earlier), like a backlogged lobby; both players get a push with the opponent's profile, accept or decline, then agree on a time to battle within the next ~6 hours.

**Critique of that proposal, and what was changed:**
- **Scheduling defers the simultaneity problem rather than removing it.** The battle itself still needs both people live at the same moment. Committing in advance moves the failure mode from "never matched" to "cleared my evening, they didn't show," which is worse for morale. Correspondence formats (chess, Words with Friends) work async because the *gameplay* is async — a roast battle can't be.
- **LIFO ("whoever queued last") is backwards** — it starves the longest-waiting player, who gets skipped forever as fresher entries arrive. Closest-rating-first with longest-waiting as the tiebreak is correct, and is already what `pollMatchmaking` does.
- **Accept/decline on every pairing is a free skip**, which makes the 3/day skip cap meaningless and re-opens opponent cherry-picking.
- **Multi-step flows compound drop-off**: queue → notify → view profile → accept → propose times → confirm → wait hours → both show up. Even at generous per-step completion rates, very few queues would produce an actual battle.

**DECIDED design — three layers:**
1. **Sixes and Sevens is the primary fix** (see the Daily "prime time" notification entry in Core Match Flow). Concentrating demand in time beats matching across time, costs nothing, and needs no new matchmaking mechanics.
2. **Standing challenges — the developer's idea, minus the calendar.** Queue as now; if nothing matches within ~90 seconds you don't fail, you leave a **standing challenge that outlives the app being closed**. Someone queueing hours later matches it instantly. Both players get the push with the opponent's profile, exactly as proposed. Then, instead of negotiating a time: **"X accepted — battle now?"** Both tap ready and the match starts; if either doesn't respond within ~5 minutes the challenge returns to the pool for someone else. This gives the backlogged-lobby feel without anyone opening a calendar. **Scheduling is reserved for tournaments**, where an entry fee and a bracket justify the coordination cost — a casual ranked match doesn't.
3. **Availability stated once, not per match.** A coarse "usually free: evenings / late night" field on the profile, which matchmaking prefers to overlap. No per-pairing time negotiation UI, and no drop-off from one.

**BUILT AND VERIFIED END TO END.** A wait that finds no live opponent now becomes a standing challenge that outlives the app being closed; someone queueing hours later matches it instantly and the challenger is woken by push. Verified against the deployed backend — an entry left 45 minutes earlier, its client long since stopped polling, was paired by a fresh queuer (11 live checks), plus 19 local tests on the rules and 39 on pairing.
- **Three rules stop this feeling worse than an empty queue.** A live opponent ALWAYS outranks a standing one, since a live player can battle in thirty seconds while a sleeper may take minutes or never answer. A challenge expires after roughly an evening, so nobody is paired against someone who has forgotten they queued. And — the one the tests forced out — **a player with no registered device NEVER becomes standing**: being pushed is the entire mechanism, so an unwakeable entry would sit in the pool being matched and never answered, costing everyone who took it up a five-minute wait for nothing. A pool of ghosts is worse than an empty pool, because the app feels *broken* rather than quiet.
- **Unanswered challenges are released** by a two-minute sweep. Without it, one sleeping player quietly removes a *willing* player from the pool: the match sits pending forever and their queue entry stays flagged `matched`, so they cannot be paired with anyone else either. Nobody is forfeited — a forfeit is for accepting and then not turning up, a promise broken rather than one never made — so the absentee keeps their challenge and the player who showed goes straight back to searching, with a push telling them why.
- **Client side**: past 90 seconds the matchmaking screen stops implying you must wait and offers leaving instead ("Your challenge is out there... you can close the app"), with Cancel still surrendering the place. A back gesture **stands down rather than cancelling**, so swiping away no longer destroys the challenge — `findMatch` gained a `standDown` future for exactly this, since its `finally` previously left the queue unconditionally. The returning banner reads "Someone took up your challenge" for standing-origin matches, because "a match is waiting" reads as a bug to someone who queued hours ago and forgot.
- **Two existing tests asserted the OLD behaviour** — that a long wait was pruned and its owner could not pair. That was exactly the problem being fixed, so they now assert the opposite. A deliberate reversal, not a regression.
- **Fixed along the way**: `PreMatchScreen`'s documented 28px overflow. On a short device the Ready button fell off the bottom entirely, stranding someone one tap short of a match with no way to reach it. The panel now scrolls (and its `Spacer` became a `SizedBox`, since a Spacer inside a scroll view has no bounded height to divide).

**Why this was a smaller build than it sounded**: the RTDB queue, the pairing rules (same-tier first, ±1 tier widening every 30s, two-way blocking, repeat-opponent cooldown, closest-rating selection) and the match-found push all already exist. Two things are missing: **queue entries must persist rather than being pruned when the client stops polling**, and **pairing must be discoverable by push hours later** rather than only by an active poll. Note that matched entries are already exempt from stale-entry pruning and `getActiveMatch` + `_ActiveMatchBanner` already handle cold-start recovery, so a good deal of the persistence machinery is in place.

**Guardrails to carry over when building this**: a **declined challenge must cost a skip** (so the existing 3/day cap keeps doing its job), and **accepted-then-no-show must count as a forfeit** — the rule already written for async tournament rounds.

**Interaction worth remembering**: the vote-confidence weighting (see functions/rating.js) means thin early matches move rating proportionally less, so the cold-start period won't bake a garbage ladder in before there's enough volume to judge properly.

## Matchmaking Queue Architecture — DECIDED
- Firestore (the primary planned database) is not well-suited to real-time "who's currently waiting to be matched" queue logic — it's built for document storage, not low-latency ephemeral state.
- **DECIDED**: use **Firebase Realtime Database** (a separate, complementary Firebase product optimized for live/ephemeral state) specifically for the matchmaking queue, while everything else (user profiles, matches, tournaments, etc.) stays in Firestore as originally planned.
- **IMPLEMENTATION STATUS — BUILT, deployed, and verified end-to-end against the real backend.** This closes the largest remaining functional gap in the app: before this, there was no way to be paired with a stranger at all, since every match joined a single hardcoded `'test-channel'`.
  - **The RTDB instance had to be created out-of-band.** `firebase database:instances:create` refuses until a default instance exists, and `firebase init database` is interactive, so it was created via the Firebase Database Management REST API (`POST firebasedatabase.googleapis.com/v1beta/projects/{p}/locations/us-central1/instances`) using the website's existing Admin service-account key. That first required a **one-time manual enable of the Realtime Database Management API** in the GCP console — the developer had to click it, since the service account lacks `serviceusage.services.enable`. Same class of one-time console step as Firestore, Storage, and Cloud Vision before it. Instance URL: `https://the-bully-league-default-rtdb.firebaseio.com`, region `us-central1`.
  - **Queue shape**: `matchmakingQueue/{mode}/{uid}` — one queue per mode, so an exhibition player is never paired into a rated match. `database.rules.json` **denies all client access to the entire tree**; only the Cloud Functions (Admin SDK, which bypasses rules) ever touch it. That's deliberate and load-bearing: a queue entry carries the player's real rating, tier, and block list, so a client-written entry would let a modified client claim any rating it liked and hand-pick opponents.
  - **Three callables drive it** (`functions/matchmaking.js`, exported from `index.js`): `enterMatchmakingQueue` (builds the entry from the caller's real Firestore user doc, refuses non-`active` accounts), `pollMatchmaking` (the pairing attempt), `leaveMatchmakingQueue` (idempotent).
  - **The client POLLS rather than subscribing to RTDB** — an explicit design choice, not an oversight. The tier-widening rule is time-based, so something has to re-attempt pairing on a timer regardless; polling makes that the same mechanism instead of a second one. It also keeps `firebase_database` off the Flutter side entirely, avoiding another Android dependency in a toolchain this doc documents as fragile (the AGP pin, the `permission_handler`/`image_picker` conflicts). Cost is one invocation per waiting client every 3s — fine at private-beta volume; the upgrade path if that ever matters is an RTDB listener for the *notification* only, keeping these callables for the pairing itself.
  - **Pairing runs as a single RTDB transaction over the whole per-mode queue node.** Transacting on the parent (rather than per-entry) is what makes the two-sided claim atomic — two separate single-entry transactions could interleave into A-claims-B while C-claims-A. The documented scaling limit is that every attempt reads and rewrites that node; sharding by tier band is the natural fix later.
  - **Rules implemented, matching the decisions already in this doc**: same-tier first, widening by ±1 tier per 30s of waiting so nobody waits forever; two-way blocking (a block is honoured from *either* side, or it would be useless); repeat-opponent cooldown as a **preference, not a filter** (falls back to a repeat rather than stranding the only two people in the queue, per the existing decision that availability takes priority); closest-rating selection with longest-waiting as the tie-break; and pruning of entries abandoned by a crashed client so they can't be paired against.
  - **`recentOpponentIds` is now actually written** (by `completeMatch`, as a `{opponentUid: timestampMs}` map), which is what makes the cooldown real rather than schema-only. The code tolerates the legacy plain-array shape too.
  - **Match documents moved server-side, and this is a real security tightening.** They're now created by `pollMatchmaking` at *pairing* time with `status: 'pending'`, and flipped to `completed` by a new `completeMatch` callable. `firestore.rules` for `matches/{matchId}` went from "either participant may create" to **`allow write: if false`**. The old arrangement let a modified client invent a match between arbitrary players and declare it finished — which is the entry point to the voting pipeline and, for ranked, to real Elo changes, i.e. exactly the class of sensitive write the Security & Compliance Baseline says belongs behind a Cloud Function.
  - **Because match docs now exist before a match is played, everything downstream had to learn about the lifecycle**: `castVote` refuses anything not `completed`; `finalizeMatch` settles a still-`pending` match found by the hourly sweep as `abandoned` with no winner and no rating change (safe, because the sweep only looks at documents already older than the full 24h vote window, so nothing in progress can be caught); and the website's Recent/Trending feeds filter to `status == 'completed'` so in-progress and abandoned matches never surface publicly. The two feed queries needed new composite indexes (`firestore.indexes.json`, previously empty).
  - **`generateAgoraToken` now validates the channel**, closing a gap its own doc comment had flagged as acceptable only while every match shared a test room. Exactly two shapes mint: `precheck_{callerUid}` (the caller's own solo pre-match camera/mic check) and `match_{matchId}` where the caller is one of that match's two players and it hasn't ended. Without this, any signed-in user could mint a token for any channel name and **drop in on a stranger's live match uninvited** — a real eavesdropping hole the moment channels stopped being a shared test room.
  - **The pre-match check now joins its own solo `precheck_{uid}` channel** rather than the real match channel, and runs BEFORE queueing rather than after. Both orderings satisfy "both users must pass this before the match starts"; doing it first means someone sorting out their lighting or a denied permission isn't burning an already-paired opponent's time.
  - **A race fix from step 9a became structurally unnecessary.** Live in-match frame sampling previously couldn't start until an `'identity'` data-channel message delivered the opponent's Firebase uid, because the auto-filed content-violation report needs someone to file against. The pairing now supplies that uid before `MatchScreen` is even built, so sampling starts at join and the race is impossible by construction rather than merely ordered around. The `'identity'` and `'matchSaved'` messages are gone. Violation-ended matches now also settle explicitly as `abandoned` (and the auto-filed report finally carries a real `matchId`, which used to be `null` because nothing was written until verdict time — which a violation never reached).
  - **Testing — two layers, both real.** (1) `functions/test/matchmaking.test.js` runs the pairing rules as pure functions with no emulator or credentials (21 checks, including **2,000 randomised concurrent-queue trials** asserting no player is ever double-booked and every match has exactly two mutually-agreeing participants). This deliberately repeats the approach that caught the tournament bye bug before it reached a device. Run with `node test/matchmaking.test.js` from `functions/`. (2) A scripted end-to-end run against the **deployed** backend with two genuine signed-in accounts confirmed the whole loop: one player queues and correctly stays searching; the second queues and pairs; the first's next poll finds the *same* match rather than creating a second; the document exists as `pending` with both players; token minting succeeds for one's own channels and is **refused** for another user's pre-check channel, for the old `test-channel`, and for a finished match; voting is refused mid-match; `completeMatch` settles it and a duplicate call is a no-op; and the cooldown record is written.
  - **Real bug found by live testing and fixed**: `enterQueue` originally rejected anyone whose user document didn't say `accountStatus === "active"`, which **locked every pre-Build-Order-step-9 account out of matchmaking entirely** — accounts created before that step introduced the field have no value at all. Caught on a real device: `PlayerTwo` (a legacy test account, `accountStatus` genuinely `undefined` in Firestore) hit "This account can't join matches right now" at the queue, while the newer `SeventhVoter` sailed through. This is the exact same trap `firestore.rules` already documents for direct field access on legacy documents, and it was also inconsistent with the app's own ban gate (`lib/app.dart` checks `== 'banned'`, not `!= 'active'`). Fixed so an **absent** status counts as active and only an explicit non-active value blocks; a banned account is still refused because its status is explicitly `'banned'`. Covered by three regression checks in the test file.
  - **Live app verification — COMPLETE, two real devices, whole loop.** Two emulators signed in as genuinely different accounts (`SeventhVoter` 1200 and `PlayerTwo` 1214, both Average Joe so same tier) each went Home → recording consent → pre-match check → queue, and **paired with each other**. Confirmed at every stage: the pre-match check joins its own solo `precheck_{uid}` channel with a real server-minted token (Agora logged `connectionChangedJoinSuccess` on `precheck_ZwqzgXjtme…`, direct proof the tightened `generateAgoraToken` accepts a legitimate pre-check channel); the matchmaking screen polls with the tier-widening message updating live as the wait grows; each device then showed the *other* player's bio reveal (PlayerTwo's device rendered SeventhVoter's real profession/education/hometown/interests; SeventhVoter's device correctly rendered the empty-profile fallback copy, since PlayerTwo has no profile); tapping Ready flipped that device to "Ready - waiting for opponent"; and both landed in `MatchScreen` **on the same `match_{matchId}` channel with bidirectional video actually flowing** — each screen showed the other's camera feed with its own as picture-in-picture, and the "End My Turn" button appeared only on the device whose turn it was. The match then played to its verdict and the client settled it server-side: the document came out `status: 'completed'`, `readyPlayerIds: [SeventhVoter, PlayerTwo]`, `completedAt` set.
  - **A testing trap worth remembering**: one earlier run looked like a real bug — one device sat in the bio reveal while the other jumped straight into the match. The cause was that an `adb install` inside a polling loop had silently not taken, leaving that device on the **previous build** (which had no bio reveal). Confirmed by comparing `dumpsys package … lastUpdateTime` across both devices, which differed by a day. When two devices behave inconsistently, check the installed build timestamps before debugging the code.
  - **Minor UI issue seen and left alone**: `PreMatchScreen` overflows by 28px on a 320×640 screen (Flutter's yellow-and-black overflow banner). Only reproduced on an unusually small emulator, and the debug-only "Skip mic check" button is part of what pushes it over, so it likely won't appear on a real device or in a release build — but it's a real layout fragility worth a look.
  - **Notable deviation from this doc's own expectations**: the four new 2nd-gen functions did **not** need the manual Cloud Run "Allow public access" fix that every previously-deployed callable required. Verified rather than assumed — an unauthenticated POST to each returned the function's own `{"status":"UNAUTHENTICATED","message":"Must be signed in."}` (the app-level `request.auth` gate) rather than a Cloud Run-level 401. So `firebase deploy` set the IAM binding correctly this time. Worth checking the same way rather than reflexively applying the manual fix on future deploys.
  - **Built immediately after, in follow-up passes**: the **pre-match bio reveal**, **skip/decline**, and the **match-found push notification** — see the Core Match Flow section for each one's status. Still NOT built: the gate requiring a few exhibition matches before Ranked unlocks (there's no `exhibitionMatchesPlayed` counter to gate on). ## Tech Stack (Decided)
- **Frontend**: Flutter (cross-platform, Android-first build, iOS later). Chosen over React Native for native performance (important for video) and Windows-friendly Android tooling.
- **Backend**: Firebase — Auth (phone/email/password), Firestore (database), **Realtime Database (specifically for the live matchmaking queue — see Matchmaking Queue Architecture above)**, Cloud Functions (server-side logic — also used to guard sensitive writes like rating/points, see Security & Compliance Baseline), Cloud Storage (recorded videos, avatars), Cloud Messaging (push notifications), **Firebase Analytics (DECIDED — free, built into existing stack, tracks usage data needed to actually tune the many "adjust later based on real data" decisions throughout this plan: round length, points balance, tier thresholds, etc.)**. Chosen specifically because developer has no backend experience — removes need to run/manage own servers.
- **Live video**: Agora (agora_rtc_engine Flutter SDK). Chosen over LiveKit for better beginner tutorials/examples and generous free tier (~10,000 min/month).
  - **IMPORTANT ARCHITECTURE RULE**: Video provider logic MUST be isolated behind an abstract `VideoCallService` interface (see Architecture below) so Agora can be swapped for LiveKit or another provider later without touching UI/match-flow/ranking code. Do not call Agora SDK directly from UI widgets — always go through the service interface.

## Testing — two layers, and why both exist
- **`functions/test/*.test.js` — pure, no emulator, no credentials.** Policy and arithmetic extracted into pure functions and exercised with plain `node`. This is what caught the tournament bye collision, the standing-challenge ghost entry, and the `Number(null)` epoch bug before any of them reached a device.
- **`functions/live/*.js` — the real deployed backend and real Firestore.** Run manually. They create throwaway accounts, exercise the real callables, assert the real effects, and delete everything they made in a `finally` block so a mid-run failure still cleans up.
- **Both are needed, and the reason is specific: this project keeps getting bitten in the SEAM between things, where a pure test cannot see.**
  - `publishOnlineCount` threw on every run because `MODES` was never exported — its pure helper had 12 passing tests, and the break was in the import.
  - `voteReminders` needed a composite index that did not exist. It would have thrown every run and been swallowed by the scheduler's own try/catch, appearing as a job that silently did nothing.
  - `FieldValue` was used but never imported in `matchFinalization.js`. `require()` succeeded because the reference is only reached at runtime inside a transaction, so every ranked finalization would have thrown.
  - **A green deploy proves none of this. Only running the thing does.**
- **`functions/live/coreLoop.js` is the critical-path regression**: queue → pair → complete → vote → finalize, then rating, wins/losses, both points numbers, rating history, rank change, entitlement, clip eligibility and the judge feed. **Run it after touching `enterQueue`, `pollMatchmaking`, `completeMatch`, `finalizeMatch` or `awardPoints`** — each is depended on by several features, and a regression there breaks the whole app regardless of what is built on top. 18 checks; last run green after a session that changed several of those paths.

## Monetization Model — REVISED AND DECIDED (supersedes the original Premium Subscription plan below)

**The one-line version, and it should stay this legible in the product too: "Free to watch. Free to battle ranked at 6pm. Subscribe to battle whenever."**

The developer's stated goal for this pass was explicit and reframes everything: **maximum profit, and/or an acquisition in the low millions.** The earlier plan (subscription for side features, everything core free forever) was re-opened against that goal and deliberately made harder.

### The model
- **Everyone starts on a 14-day full-access free trial. No card at signup.**
- **When the trial ends you keep two things forever**: full **Spectator** access (watch, judge, vote, the whole feed), and the ability to **battle RANKED during Sixes and Sevens** — the daily 6-7pm Pacific window.
- **The subscription buys battling OUTSIDE the window**, plus the feature bundle below.
- **Price**: start at **$6.99/mo, $39.99/yr**, and treat both as tunable. 2026 benchmarks put the median monthly subscription at **$6.68** and $9.99 in the *top quartile*, not the middle; Gaming's annual median is **$20.55**. The target demo is college-age, so pricing at or under the median is the right starting point.

### Why NOT a hard paywall — the reasoning, so it is not re-litigated
The developer asked directly whether a hard paywall could work, given published data that hard paywalls convert roughly **5x** better than freemium (freemium free-to-paid runs **2-5%**; social networking is the **worst-converting iOS category at 9.8%**). The answer is no, for one structural reason:

- **This app is a MARKETPLACE, not a tool.** A meditation app or a photo editor delivers value to one person sitting alone, so a paywall merely filters customers and the product still works for whoever pays. The Bully League delivers nothing without an opponent *and* an audience. A paywall there does not tax demand, it destroys supply. The "5x better" figure is measured on products that work fine for a solo buyer.
- **The decisive specific: a hard paywall kills the JUDGES.** Rating only moves as far as a match is actually judged, and participants cannot vote on their own match. If every user is a paying battler, judges must come from other subscribers — who are busy battling. Vote confidence would sit permanently low, every result would be discounted, and **subscribers would be paying for a ladder that barely moves.** Any paywall design must answer "who votes?" first.
- **Sequencing, not principle — the Xbox Live lesson.** Console multiplayer IS hard-paywalled and hugely profitable, but Xbox Live launched into an install base of millions of consoles. **A paywall on a multiplayer product is a harvesting mechanism, not a starting one.** Revisit once liquidity exists; it is wrong at zero users.
- **It also points away from the acquisition goal.** At "a few million," subscription apps trade around **3-8x ARR** ($3M ≈ $500K-1M ARR ≈ 6-12k subscribers). Social apps get bought on **users and engagement**, and the assets that make this acquirable — the network, the match archive, the TikTok following — are exactly what a paywall suppresses.

### Two developer proposals considered and REJECTED, with reasons
- **"Free users get practice only; paid users on Ranked get paired into free users' practice, rated for the payer only."** Clever — it keeps free users as opponent supply rather than deleting them. Rejected because **asymmetric stakes corrupt the exact thing being sold**: a player with nothing on the line behaves differently (less prepared, quicker to quit), so subscribers' ratings would be built largely on matches against unmotivated opponents, making the ladder noise. It also cheapens the win at the emotional peak for both sides, and it makes throwaway free accounts *useful* as a farming input where today they are worthless.
- **"5 free practice rounds, then subscribe."** A metered trial rather than a free tier. Rejected for launch because **it kills the cold start**: the growth loop is clips → TikTok → install → battle → more clips, and a 5-round meter means almost no early matches, so almost no clips, so the loop never spins up. It also kills word of mouth, since "come roast me" does not survive a friend hitting a paywall. Viable *later*, not at launch.
- **Why TIME-based beat USAGE-based**, which is the key insight: a usage meter punishes the most engaged users **fastest** — they hit the wall in one sitting, before any habit forms. A time trial does the opposite: the wall arrives *after* the habit exists. For a product built on a daily ritual, that is the right shape.
- **Why NO CARD at signup**, despite card-gated trials converting at **48.8%** against **18.2%** for no-card. Per 100 installs the revenue lands in a similar place, but no-card leaves roughly **80 active battlers instead of 25**. In an app where free users are infrastructure, that difference is the whole game — and asking a TikTok click-through for a card before their first roast poisons the cheapest acquisition channel there is.
- **The known structural risk of this model, accepted with eyes open**: a trial population **expires** where a freemium free tier **accumulates**. That makes battler liquidity a function of acquisition rate rather than a compounding reservoir. **The Sixes and Sevens carve-out is the specific mitigation** — it gives lapsed users a permanent reason to battle, concentrated into the hour where concentration is wanted anyway.

### Why the free hour is RANKED, not unranked
- **The window exists to make ONE crowd at ONE time.** Free-unranked plus paid-ranked would split the pool into two thinner queues during the single hour designed to unsplit it — matchmaking would get *worse* at peak.
- **You convert people who have something at stake, not people who have nothing.** A free player sitting at 1340 with a rank title has something to protect and a ladder to climb. Nothing-at-stake is a churn condition, not a conversion condition — which is precisely what sank the rejected proposal above.
- A dedicated free player could in principle reach the top on one hour a day. **That is allowed**: a free player in the GOAT five is a story, and they would be the most motivated subscriber in the app the moment their spot was threatened. The **prize** ceiling stays paid regardless, since tournaments are where money lives.

### Feature decisions
- **WATERMARK EVERYTHING — reverses the earlier "paid gets the unwatermarked clip" decision.** TikTok is the primary growth channel and the whole content strategy depends on clips spreading **with attribution**. Selling watermark removal means paying your most engaged, most-likely-to-post users to strip your branding — charging money to weaken your own distribution. Keep the mark tasteful and sell *access to the clip*, never attribution.
- **Auto-rendered clips are the flagship paid feature.** Rendering costs roughly **$0.10** a match and is currently admin-only, so most players cannot see or share their own battles. It is the ideal paid feature because it **genuinely costs money to provide** (recovering real spend rather than inventing scarcity), serves **status and sharing** which is what this audience is actually here for, has **zero competitive impact**, and **feeds growth** because subscribers post their clips.
  - **The distinction is AUTOMATIC vs REQUESTED, not free vs paid.** Auto-rendering every free match would cost roughly **$156k/year** at 10k users; renders people actually asked for cost a few thousand. So subscribers get **every** ranked match rendered without thinking about it; everyone else buys or earns individual renders.
- **Stats/analytics** — rating history, win rate by opponent tier, head-to-head, round-level breakdowns. Serves skill improvement, which is a real motivation for this audience.
- **Tournament economics** — reduced entry fees, a set number of free entries per month, members-only tournaments and giveaways. Ties the subscription to the prize motivation and to the entry-fee revenue stream rather than competing with it.
- **Player directory** — search and browse players, see a thumbnail and limited profile, challenge specific people (**exhibition only**, per the Friend Battles constraint). **REQUIRED alongside it: a "don't list me" opt-out, and the directory MUST respect blocks.** A searchable database of photos in an app whose premise is insulting strangers is a harassment surface — someone can find a specific person and repeatedly challenge them. Cheap now, awkward to retrofit after the first complaint.
- **Skips: 3/day free, 6/day subscriber, hard-capped, never in tournaments.** Note the reason, because an earlier loss-aversion argument for this was **wrong and the developer correctly rejected it** — nothing has launched, so nothing is being taken away. The real reason skips stay scarce for *everyone* including subscribers is that **every skip costs the other player**, who queued, got paired, sat through a bio reveal and got declined. In a thin pool that is expensive. The competitive-integrity angle is secondary: Elo is self-correcting, so dodging strong opponents mostly avoids losses rather than inflating rating.
- **Opponent rating/rank stays HIDDEN pre-match for everyone, including subscribers.** Selling it was proposed and rejected: it reverses a deliberate design decision, creates **asymmetric information inside a rated match**, and is concretely gameable — knowing an opponent's rank tells you exactly when to spend a skip, so combined with a skip perk it would amount to selling rating manipulation. The industry converged on cosmetics-and-convenience-only precisely because power purchases break competitive integrity, and research finds the people most likely to buy an edge are **low-to-mid win-rate players** — i.e. exactly the cohort whose rank would become fictional.
- **THE RULE THAT GOVERNS ALL FUTURE PAID FEATURES: nothing between pairing and verdict is ever different between tiers.** The pre-match ammo reveal in particular is identical for both — it IS the core mechanic, and a paid player with ammo against a free player without is not a comedy contest, it is a purchase.
- **Practice matches are capped for free users** outside the window (exact number still open, and largely moot now that the ranked unlock gate is removed — see below). Practice costs about **$0.02** a match in Agora and produces no clip, no votes and no content, so the cap's job is funnelling into Ranked, not cost control.

Every check passes while the flag is off.

Signup *does* write it, so new accounts track properly — but this is the third time this project has met the missing-field trap, after `accountStatus` and the `firestore.rules` direct-access bug, and it is now pinned by a regression test.

- **The ranked unlock gate is GONE** — `rankedUnlockState`, `EXHIBITION_MATCHES_TO_UNLOCK_RANKED`, the `getRankedUnlock` callable (deleted from the deployed project), its Dart client and the Home progress UI are all removed. Home now shows a plain "Find Ranked Match".

- **THE CLIENT NOW KNOWS ITS OWN STATE — BUILT.** `getMyEntitlement` + `EntitlementService` + a `_TrialStatus` line on Home.
  - **The important part is WHERE the check happens.** Previously a lapsed player would tap Ranked, sit through the tutorial gate, the recording consent screen and the entire camera-and-mic check, and only *then* be turned away by `enterQueue`. That is the worst possible moment to say no — real effort has been spent, and the refusal reads as a bug rather than a price. `_startMatch` now asks first, so it becomes an offer instead of a late rejection. The server still enforces; the client check is a convenience and never a boundary.
  - **The blocked sheet is never a dead end.** Practice-closed-during-window points at Ranked, which is free for everyone right then; lapsed-outside-window points at the window, free tonight. Both are real alternatives available today rather than a bare subscribe button — a paywall with no free path is how an app teaches people to close it. It does **not** promise a purchase flow, because there is no IAP to send anyone to.
  - **The trial countdown is the conversion mechanism, not decoration**: a trial only converts if people know it is ending. Equally, a lapsed player is told what they still *have* (ranked, free, every night) rather than just losing access and guessing why.
  - **Renders NOTHING while enforcement is off**, which is the current state — counting down a trial that expires into no restriction at all would be a threat the app has no intention of carrying out.
  - **Fails OPEN**: an unreachable check reports full access, so a network blip can never look like a paywall.
  - **7 live checks** against the deployed backend across trial, lapsed, in-window and out-of-window states.

Surfaced because a verification run happened to execute at 23:00 Pacific and the window would not arm. The END may now be **24**, meaning midnight. Irrelevant to 6-7pm, but it was a silent wrong answer rather than an error.
  - **Mirrored in `lib/core/services/event_window.dart` in the same change**, because these are two implementations of one window: if they drift the app counts down to one time while the server notifies at another, and nothing would surface it. 28 Dart tests still pass.
  - The window still cannot **wrap** past midnight; a late-night window would need a second range, which nothing has asked for.
- **NOT built yet**: any IAP or subscription purchase flow, and the `subscription` field being written by anything other than a hand edit. The server will honour `subscription.active` the moment something sets it.

### When to switch charging on
**Decide the boundary now, charge later.** The "subscriptions from day one" advice the developer received is really about deciding what is free *before habits form*, because clawing back a free feature is the most damaging pricing move available. That part is done here. But today the paid tier would be: search a directory with no players, reduced fees on tournaments that do not run, and members-only events with no members. **Switch on billing once the beta proves the loop and the tournament economy actually exists.**

- **Legal note (unchanged)**: a feature-access subscription is NOT a sweepstakes/contest-law question like tournament entry fees are — it is a standard SaaS-style subscription, cleanly handled via Apple/Google's built-in subscription APIs.

## What Is Actually Being Sold — CORRECTION, then BUILT
**An earlier cost estimate in this conversation was wrong, and the correction changes the product.** The claim was that auto-rendering every free user's match would cost roughly **$156k/year** at 10k users, at ~$0.10 a render. That is not what the pipeline does. `functions/autoRender.js` already renders **every** recorded match, and that stage costs about **$0.003** — the ~$0.066 is **transcription**, which is why captions are already rationed to a weekly top-N. Universal basic rendering therefore costs closer to **$4.7k/year** at that scale, and it is already happening, because in-app judging needs something to watch.

**So the scarce, expensive, genuinely valuable artifact is the CAPTIONED clip, not the clip.** That is also exactly what makes a clip work on TikTok, Reels and Shorts, where most viewing is muted — the Production Quality Bar section already says so. Selling captions is honest: it is the part that costs real money and the part worth posting. Withholding the cheap uncaptioned render would be artificial scarcity **and would break judging**.

- **Free**: every ranked match gets a clip, watchable in-app.
- **Entitled**: the **captioned, downloadable** version — via subscription, points, or (later) purchase.

**BUILT AND VERIFIED — `functions/clipGrants.js` + the `requestMatchClip` callable.** Three ways to become entitled, one code path, so "is this person a subscriber" has exactly one definition (it reuses `battleEntitlement`).
- **POINTS BUY A CAPTIONED CLIP — this is the points economy's first real sink, and it answers the open "what do points buy" question.** It satisfies the principle that a sink must be something people genuinely want AND something that costs the platform real money, or the currency is monopoly money. It is also the **free-tier on-ramp to the flagship paid feature**, so whoever grinds for it is a pre-qualified subscriber. Default price **250 points**, in `config/pointsSettings.clipPrice` and bounds-checked, since CLAUDE.md is explicit that economy numbers need playtesting.
- **TWO NUMBERS, and the split is what makes spending compatible with the existing design.** `points` is the **career total and still only ever rises** — the progression ladder, and the property `points.js` exists to guarantee. `pointsBalance` is what is left to spend. Without the split, spending would quietly turn points into a second rating, which is the one thing they were designed not to be.
  - **A missing `pointsBalance` inherits the career total**, because an account predating spending has by definition spent nothing. Reading it as zero would confiscate every point earned so far — the same missing-field trap as `accountStatus` and `createdAt`, now met for the fourth time and pinned by tests on both the award and spend paths. Both write the balance **absolutely rather than by increment** for exactly this reason.
- **Granted clips jump the weekly caption ranking** via the `highlight.captionRequested` flag `autoRender` already honours. Someone who paid must never depend on a popularity contest.
- **Delivery is deferred past the objection window**, per the developer's own solution: buy at the end of the match when the feeling is strongest, receive once the other player's chance to object has closed. Nothing revocable is ever delivered, so there is **no refund problem to solve** — which matters because the refund support category was deliberately removed, Apple and Google owning that process. `clipDeliverable` reports `refundable: true` only for an actual objection, distinguishing "something went wrong, make them whole" from "it is simply not time yet".
- **Participants only** — enforced as a safety boundary, not a pricing one.
- **Repeat taps are idempotent**, returning "already yours" rather than charging again. Spending goes through a deterministic ledger entry inside a transaction, mirroring `awardPoints`, because a double charge is far worse than a double credit.
- **`firestore.rules` now also protects `pointsBalance` and `subscription`** — a client that could write either would mint itself unlimited captioned clips or simply grant itself the paid tier.
- **Verified against the deployed backend** with three real accounts and a real match: an outsider is refused, a lapsed participant cannot claim the subscriber perk, purchase is refused explicitly rather than silently treated as free, points buy the clip and draw the balance down by exactly the price while the career total is untouched, captions are requested, a repeat tap charges nothing, the other participant is told the price, a subscriber gets it at zero cost, a purchase inside an open objection window returns `deliverable: false` with a time, and a direct client write to `pointsBalance` is refused 403. **13 live checks plus 20 local tests.**
- **Hit the documented Cloud Run 401 on the newly-created callable; a plain redeploy fixed it**, exactly as CLAUDE.md's earlier note predicted.
- **REFUND-TO-CREDIT — BUILT and verified.** `refundClipGrants` runs inside `requestTakedown`: if an objection kills a clip somebody paid for, points go straight back to the spendable balance and a (future) purchase becomes a `clipCredits` token. **An in-app credit, never a store refund** — the refund support category was deliberately removed because Apple and Google own that process, so a refund path here could only route someone somewhere we cannot help. The **objector is never refunded**, or objecting would become a way to get clips free. Idempotent on the ledger id and marked with `refundedAt`, so a repeated takedown never pays twice, and **best-effort**: a refund failure must never block a takedown, which is the urgent, rights-affecting half. 9 live checks against the deployed backend, including that the career total is untouched throughout and that a second takedown refunds nothing.
- **CLIENT UI — BUILT.** `GetClipSheet` (`lib/screens/battles/get_clip_sheet.dart`), reached from a film icon on each completed recorded battle in My Battles — the screen already showing your own battles, and already the home of the takedown flow. Shows the points price against your live balance, or "Included with your subscription", and states plainly that a clip bought before voting closes is held until the opponent's chance to object has passed ("that is the same protection you get"). Offered only for recorded modes, since Practice produces no footage and promising a clip there would be a lie.
  - **The copy says "the captioned cut", never "your clip"** — every ranked battle already gets a clip, so claiming to sell one would read as a con the first time somebody spotted their own battle in the feed. It sells what is actually scarce, and explains why captions matter ("most people watch with the sound off").
  - The sheet mirrors `spendableBalance`'s legacy rule client-side, falling back to the career total when no balance field exists, so an older account is never told it is broke when it is not.
- **DELIVERY ENDPOINT — BUILT and verified.** `getClipDownload` hands over a **1-hour signed URL** per rendition, with `promptSaveAs` so it saves rather than streams. A Download button appears in `GetClipSheet` once a clip is owned; it prefers the **vertical** cut, since that is what people post and the landscape one is a letterboxed strip on a phone.
  - **The objection check on this path is the point, not a formality.** `publishHighlight` already refuses while an objection stands, but a self-serve download would have walked straight around it and let someone post a clip their opponent had explicitly objected to. **The gate has to sit on every path that hands over bytes**, and this is one.
  - **Signed URLs rather than a Firebase download token**, deliberately: a token mints a PERMANENT public URL, so for an unreviewed clip anyone it was forwarded to would have it forever. Signing keeps `storage.rules` denying clients all read on `match_highlights/**` and makes this callable the only door.
  - **A real UX bug was caught by the live test.** After a refund the grant is marked `refundedAt`, and the endpoint lumped that in with "no grant" — so a buyer whose clip had been taken down was told "You haven't got this clip yet", which reads as the purchase having silently failed. It now reports `refunded` with the actual reason and the fact that they were already made whole. The test initially asserted the *blocking*, which passed for the wrong reason; the message is what needed fixing.
  - **10 live checks against the deployed backend**, using a real object in Storage so the URL was genuinely fetched: no grant is refused, buying then downloading works, the signed URL returns the real bytes **with no credentials**, it arrives as an attachment, a non-participant is refused, an objection blocks it and the buyer is refunded, and a clip still awaiting captions is refused.
  - **`url_launcher` was added and de-risked with a real Android build before being relied on**, per this project's documented AGP/KGP fragility. Builds clean against the pinned AGP 8.9.1 / KGP 2.1.0.
- **WATERMARK — BUILT and verified on the DEPLOYED runtime.** "THE BULLY LEAGUE" is burned into every rendition, top-left, sized as a fraction of canvas height so it is proportionate in both the 1920-tall vertical cut and the 1080-tall landscape one. This closes the gap the download endpoint opened: that endpoint hands people the exact file they will post to TikTok, and an unbranded clip is free distribution for somebody else's feed.
  - **Implemented as an ASS event alongside the captions, NOT as ffmpeg `drawtext`.** drawtext is the obvious answer and it is the risky one: it needs a font file, and a missing font fails the whole render — a cosmetic mark would take the entire clip pipeline down with it. Riding along with libass reuses the text path the pipeline already had.
  - **The subtitle file is now written ALWAYS, not only when there are cues**, since it carries the mark. `captioned` stays keyed to real cues rather than to the file existing, or every clip would claim captions it does not have.
  - **Top-left deliberately**: the bottom and right of a short-form frame are where TikTok, Reels and Shorts stack their own UI, and the vertical centre is where captions sit on a stacked composite. Drawn on ASS layer 1 so it is never buried under a caption.
  - **A FINDING THAT MATTERS BEYOND THE WATERMARK: libass had NEVER rendered text on the deployed runtime before this.** Captions were only ever verified locally, and a query over every match confirmed **2 rendered, 0 captioned, 0 with any cues** — every test match transcribed to silence, so the `subtitles` filter had never actually drawn anything in production. The "safe because captions already work there" reasoning was therefore resting on a false premise, and this watermark was the first thing to test it. It passed, which **also de-risks captions** for whenever real speech is finally transcribed.
  - **Verified the only way that settles it**: a real match re-rendered through the deployed `renderMatchHighlight`, the output pulled from Storage, and a frame extracted and looked at — "THE BULLY LEAGUE" is legibly in the picture. The render also succeeded with **zero cues**, proving the now-unconditional subtitles filter does not break the large majority of clips.
  - **Legibility on real footage is NOT yet confirmed** — the only available source is an emulator test pattern, which is very bright. The mark is white at ~45% opacity with a dark outline, which held up there, but it wants a look on real camera footage.
  - **Unrelated but worth recording**: match `6caX71o7xygcXhjryIKX` cannot be rendered at all ("No usable segments in the recording"). Its files include a stray `_0.mp4`, i.e. leftovers from the old composite-mode recording that the individual-mode pipeline cannot read. Not a regression; skip it when testing renders.
- **NOT built**: IAP.

## Pay-Per-Clip — NEW, DECIDED (purchase path not yet built)
**$1.99 per produced clip** (price still tunable), sold to free users as a one-off consumable IAP, with the buy button on the **post-match screen** — the highest-intent moment in the app.

- **Why it is worth building alongside the subscription**: it monetises the roughly 90% who will never subscribe, which raises blended LTV and therefore how much can be spent on acquisition — directly serving the profit goal. Margin is strong (about $1.39 net after a 30% store fee, against a ~$0.10 render). It needs no new infrastructure beyond the IAP plumbing the subscription already requires. And it doubles as an **upsell ladder**: at $6.99/mo, four purchases exceed the subscription.
- **It gates the RENDER, not the download.** The produced clip — captions burned in, both angles cut together, properly framed — is a genuinely different artifact from what a screen recording during a match would capture (the raw two-panel layout, one POV). **The developer's position, and it is correct**: the ToS grants the platform a license to use the footage and promises no produced copy in return.
  - **One compliance carve-out, not a moral objection**: UK GDPR gives users a right of *data access*, and video of a person is personal data, so a UK user can request a copy and must receive something within a month. A manual, unpolished data-access export is a completely different thing from a produced shareable clip, so this does not threaten the model — but the ToS should be worded with the carve-out rather than absolutely.
- **PARTICIPANTS ONLY.** Only the two players in a match may buy that match's clip. Explicitly confirmed by the developer. Letting spectators buy clips of other people's battles would be a materially different and much riskier product — selling video of person X to person Y is a real safety problem in an app where somebody might fixate on a specific person.
- **Purchased at the end of the match, DELIVERED once the 24-hour objection window closes.** This is the developer's own solution to the revocation problem and it is a clean one: nothing revocable is ever delivered, so the refund problem disappears entirely. It also preserves the impulse purchase at peak emotion, and the wait adds a little anticipation.
  - **If the opponent objects before delivery, the purchase converts to an in-app RENDER CREDIT** usable on a future match — never a store refund. This matters because the refund support category was deliberately removed (Apple and Google own that process), so an in-app credit is the only path that makes someone whole immediately without routing them somewhere we cannot help.
  - **A sale is blocked outright if an objection already exists** at purchase time.
  - **State the revocation possibility in one line at purchase**, so the credit conversion reads as fair rather than arbitrary.
- **The third-party limit goes in the ToS explicitly**: *"Clips downloaded by the other participant before your removal request may remain in their possession."* The position is correct and standard — once someone holds a file it cannot be recalled — but it must be a known term rather than an unpleasant discovery during a takedown request. Note this is the honest limit of ANY takedown, including a harm-channel one.
- **IMPLEMENTATION CATCH, cheaper to close before the feature exists**: a download path must be gated by the **participant-objection check**, not just `publishHighlight`. Today objections are enforced at publication; a self-serve download would walk straight around it and let someone post a clip their opponent had explicitly objected to.
- **STILL OPEN**: the exact price, and whether any renders stay free. The argument for keeping a free path (1/week, or simply the first one ever) is that clips are **distribution, not a perk** — every user-posted clip is acquisition nobody paid for, and restricting them to subscribers cuts organic reach to roughly 10% of the userbase. "First clip free" is the cheapest version: everyone experiences having a clip and posting it once, which is what creates the desire to buy the next.

Deeper competitive data (rating history over time, win rate by opponent tier, head-to-head records, round-level breakdowns) — appeals directly to the same competitive-minded users already engaged by the ranking system.
  - **RATING HISTORY IS NOW BEING RECORDED — built ahead of the dashboard, deliberately, because it is the one part that cannot be added later.** A rating update overwrites the previous value, so an entry not written at finalization time is gone permanently; nothing else in the system remembers what a rating was before a match. Every day this went unbuilt was a day of beta history that could never be recovered. The dashboard itself can be built whenever.
  - `users/{uid}/ratingHistory/{matchId}` gets one entry per player per rated match — before, after, delta, opponent, won, and the **vote confidence**, so a flat result stays explicable later ("why did that win barely move me" has an answer in the record).
  - **Written inside the SAME transaction as the rating change**, so the history can never disagree with the rating it describes. **Keyed by matchId**, so a retried finalization overwrites rather than duplicating — the same idempotence trick the points ledger uses.
  - **A subcollection, not an array on the user document.** The user doc is read constantly (every entitlement check, every Home load, every queue entry) and an unbounded array of every match ever played would bloat all of them. History is written once and read only when someone looks.
  - **`firestore.rules` allows read-own, write-never** — a forgeable history would make every stat built on it meaningless, exactly as for `rating` itself.
  - `getMyRatingHistory` serves **your own history only**: someone else's trend is competitive information, and CLAUDE.md deliberately hides opponent rating, so serving a stranger's form would walk straight around that. The summary describes **form** (climbing/sliding over a bounded recent window) rather than totals, since wins and losses are already on the profile and what a player cannot otherwise see is whether they are currently climbing.
  - **A draw breaks a streak** rather than extending either kind — neither player won, so counting it inside a winning run would be a plain lie.
  - **A real bug was caught before deploying**: `FieldValue` was used for the entry timestamp but never imported in `matchFinalization.js`. A module-load check passed because the reference is only reached at runtime *inside the transaction* — so every ranked finalization would have thrown, taking rating changes down with it. Found by checking the import rather than trusting `require()` to succeed.
  - **Verified against the deployed backend**: a real ranked match writes an entry for both players from their own side, the stored rating matches the user document exactly, the read path returns form and a streak, a retried finalization does not duplicate, and another player is refused a 403 on someone else's history. **12 live checks plus 10 local tests.**

## Match Recording — BUILT (the prerequisite the whole content pipeline was blocked on)
Until this, nothing anywhere captured any footage — the discovery feed showed metadata only, and every highlight/TikTok plan below was blocked on there being video at all.
- **Agora Cloud Recording, INDIVIDUAL mode (server-side), writing into the project's existing Cloud Storage bucket** (`functions/cloudRecording.js`). Server-side rather than on-device because it survives a client crash, a backgrounded app, or a player force-quitting — exactly the matches most worth reviewing — and costs players no upload bandwidth or battery on mobile data.
- **Individual (per-player) rather than composite, and this reversed an earlier decision.** Composite mode was built first and worked, but it bakes one layout choice into an asset that can never be recaptured: match footage is unrepeatable, so a later discovery about what performs on short-form video would strand every match recorded before it. Individual mode records each player's stream untouched and defers every framing decision to the editing pipeline, where it can be changed and re-rendered freely.
  - **It also allows different renditions from one recording**, which a single composite forbids: a **9:16 vertical stacked** cut for TikTok/Reels/Shorts trimmed to the strongest 15-60s, and a **landscape side-by-side** full-length version for the website. (Side-by-side is genuinely good in landscape and genuinely bad in vertical, where it gives each player a 540×1920 sliver — worth remembering, since it's a natural-sounding idea that fails on geometry.)
  - **Both players stay visible in both renditions, deliberately.** Speaker-only framing was considered and rejected: in roast content the listener's reaction is frequently funnier than the line.
  - **Cost is unchanged by the switch.** Agora bills on aggregate resolution across recorded streams, so two 720×1280 streams total 1,843,200 px — the same Full HD band as a single 1080×1920 composite, at the same ~$0.034 per match. Two 1080×1920 streams would total 4,147,200 px and jump to the 2K+ band at roughly four times the price, which is why the client encoder is pinned to 720×1280 (also TikTok's stated minimum, so nothing is lost in the delivered clip).
  - **Three constraints of individual mode, all found by hitting them:**
    1. `subscribeUidGroup` is **required** — omitting it is a hard 400 ("subscribeUidGroup type mismatch or missing under single mode"), not a default. `0` covers 1-2 uids.
    2. **MP4 is not supported** — "mp4, mp3, fmp4 or hls-fmp4 in avFileType is not supported by individual mode". Output is HLS only: an `.m3u8` playlist plus `.ts` segments per player, per track. This makes the editing pipeline **mandatory** rather than optional, since no directly postable file comes out of the recorder. ffmpeg reads `.m3u8` as an input, so it costs a build step, not capability.
    3. Files are named with the publisher's uid embedded (`__uid_s_1__uid_e`), which is how `uidFromFileName` attributes each file to a player.
  - **IMPORTANT for the editing pipeline: the audio tracks have GAPS.** Each player's mic is muted during their opponent's turn (by design — see the Match Structure section), so their audio track only covers the time they were actually unmuted. Measured on a real match: video covered 119.5s per player while audio covered 89.3s and 75.4s respectively. **Audio must be aligned to video using the absolute `#EXT-X-AGORA-TRACK-EVENT ... TIME=` markers in the playlists, not by assuming the tracks are contiguous or the same length.** Concatenating audio segments naively would desynchronise everything.
  - **Verified end-to-end on two real devices**: 18 files for a completed match, cleanly split into uid 1 audio/video and uid 2 audio/video, with `WIDTH=720,HEIGHT=1280,ROTATE=0` confirmed in the video playlists — so the client encoder configuration is landing exactly as set.
- **Scope honours the existing decision**: ranked and tournament only. Exhibition matches return `mode-not-recorded` — not an error, since not recording them is correct behaviour, and it keeps recording cost off the mode people play most casually.
- **Started by the host device at host-election time**, which is the first moment both players are provably in the channel. Starting at pairing would record an empty room while the second player finished their bio reveal, and Agora bills by duration. Only the host asks, so the two devices don't race; a duplicate is a no-op.
- **Recording can never block a match.** Every failure path — vendor unreachable, credentials missing, start or stop failing — is caught, recorded on the match document (`unconfigured` / `start_failed` / `stop_failed`), and the match proceeds. Stopping happens *after* the match status write for the same reason, so a slow Agora call can't strand a match as `pending`. Agora's own `maxIdleTime` also stops a recording once both players leave, so a failed stop costs the file list, not the footage.
- **SUPERSEDED by the individual-mode switch above** — the composite framing notes below are kept because the reasoning still governs how the *editing pipeline* should render its output, but no compositing happens at record time any more.
- **The composite was 1080×1920 (9:16 vertical) — CORRECTED from an earlier mistake worth recording.** It was originally 960×360, an 8:3 ultra-wide strip chosen to sit inside Agora's cheap HD billing band. That was wrong for this product: dropped into a 9:16 frame that occupies about a fifth of the screen height with the rest empty, and there is no vertical picture information to recover by cropping — it would have produced unusable clips for the exact channel (TikTok/Reels/Shorts) the entire content strategy depends on. Researched properly: TikTok's documented standard is **1080×1920 at 9:16**, minimum 720×1280.
  - **The cost step turns out to be unavoidable, which makes the decision easy.** Agora bills on aggregate resolution, and *any* genuinely vertical format at or above TikTok's minimum already lands in the Full HD band (921,600–2,073,600 px). The only vertical shape that stays in the HD band is roughly 540×960, which is *below* TikTok's stated minimum. And once you're in the Full HD band, 720×1280 and 1080×1920 cost exactly the same — so full 1080×1920 is simply the best value available. Recording goes from ~$0.015 to ~$0.034 per match (~$19 more per 1,000 recorded matches), which is negligible against the Production quality bar this doc sets elsewhere.
  - **Frame rate and bitrate were raised for free**: Agora bills on resolution and duration only, not fps or bitrate, so 15fps/800kbps (tuned for the tiny old canvas, and blocky at 1080×1920) became **30fps/2500kbps** at zero billing cost.
  - **Verified on a real match**: the produced mp4's own track header reads exactly **1080×1920, ratio 0.5625** — parsed out of the MP4 box structure directly, since no ffmpeg is available in this environment.
  - **Stacking VISUALLY CONFIRMED** by watching the produced file — the two players appear one above the other, full width. Worth remembering how this was caught: the mp4 header reported a correct 1080×1920 under *both* layouts, so automated inspection said "fine" while the arrangement inside the frame was wrong. Composite layout can only be verified by actually watching the output.
  - **Layout is an explicit stack (`mixedVideoLayout: 3` + `layoutConfig`), after "best fit" was tried and proved wrong.** Best fit (`1`) tiles participants **side by side regardless of canvas shape**, so on a 9:16 canvas each player got a narrow half-width column with dead space above and below — caught only by actually watching the output file, since the header reports the correct 1080×1920 either way. Player 1 now occupies `y_axis` 0.0–0.5 and player 2 0.5–1.0, each full width.
  - **This forced a related change: players now join with FIXED Agora uids (1 and 2) instead of the wildcard 0.** A customized layout has to name each region's occupant by uid, and the server has no way to learn a dynamically-assigned one. The uid is decided server-side at pairing (player1→1, player2→2) and carried on the pairing. The token is unaffected — it's minted against uid 0, which Agora treats as a wildcard valid for any uid. Side effect: host election (`myUid < oppUid`) is now deterministic, so **player1 always hosts** rather than it depending on whichever uid Agora happened to assign.
  - **`render_mode: 0` (crop) on both regions**, not fit. Each tile is 1080×960 — wider than tall — while phones publish portrait, so "fit" would pillarbox each player into a narrow strip with black either side. Crop fills the tile and keeps the middle of the frame, which is where the pre-match oval guide already trains players to put their face.
  - **Worth considering later**: since only one player speaks per turn in this format, switching the layout per turn (Agora supports updating layout mid-recording) to full-screen the active speaker would give each roaster the whole 1080×1920 with no cropping at all — much stronger short-form content than a static split.
- **The human review gate exists in the schema from day one**: recordings start `reviewStatus: 'pending'`, `published: false`, and nothing in this pipeline publishes anything on its own — matching the requirement below that a human approve a clip AND separately confirm it won't trip TikTok/Instagram/YouTube's rules, which are stricter than this app's internal speech policy.
- **`storage.rules` denies clients BOTH read and write on `match_recordings/**`.** Write, because nothing in the app uploads footage so any write would be forged. Read, because this is raw unreviewed video of two people — a readable path would route straight around the review gate, letting anyone who could guess a match id pull unreviewed footage of strangers. Agora writes with its own HMAC credentials, which bypass rules exactly as the Admin SDK does.
- **Credentials needed to actually switch it on** (server-side secrets, never shipped to the client): `AGORA_CUSTOMER_ID` and `AGORA_CUSTOMER_SECRET` (Agora's RESTful API pair, **separate** from the App ID/App Certificate already in use — generate in Agora Console → RESTful API), plus `RECORDING_STORAGE_ACCESS_KEY`/`RECORDING_STORAGE_SECRET_KEY` (a Cloud Storage HMAC key pair, from GCP Console → Cloud Storage → Settings → Interoperability, so Agora can write into the bucket). All four are currently set to the placeholder `unset`.
- **Runaway-recording watchdog — BUILT** (`stopRunawayRecordings`, scheduled every 10 minutes). This closes the project's only genuinely unbounded cost path. Agora's `maxIdleTime` stops a recording shortly after the channel empties, but does nothing while clients are still connected — so a wedged state machine or a client that never leaves would bill recording minutes indefinitely with nothing to notice. **A billing alert would only report that after the money was spent; this prevents the spend.** The cap is *derived per match* from the settings stamped on that match rather than being a fixed number, because timings are live-configurable: a flat cap generous enough for a 10-round/120-second format would be far too slack for the 3-round default, and one tight enough for the default would cut off a legitimate tournament format and destroy real footage. 7 local tests pin this across the entire configurable range.
  - **A real design flaw was caught by writing those tests**: the first version used 5 minutes of slack, which meant a *wedged* recording (~8 min) cost more than double a *real* match (~3 min) — the backstop was worse than the thing it guarded. Slack is now 2 minutes. The tests assert on **cost** rather than seconds, since that's the property actually being protected, and note that the 10-minute poll interval dominates anyway, so tuning slack below a couple of minutes buys nothing. Worst case at the most extreme legal configuration stays under $1; the everyday case is a few cents.
- **CONFIRMED WORKING END-TO-END on two real devices.** Credentials are set and a real ranked match produced a real recording: a **3.3 MB composited `.mp4`** of both players side by side, plus 8 HLS `.ts` segments and an `.m3u8` playlist, all under `match_recordings/{matchId}/`. The match document's `recording.files` lists all ten with sizes and content types, and flags the composite mp4 via `isComposite`. Also verified earlier with credentials still unset (the safety property): exhibition correctly skipped, ranked recorded `unconfigured` and started nothing, both completed normally.
  - **Two real bugs were found by that live test, both now fixed** — neither was reachable by the scripted tests, because both needed two genuine clients:
    1. **`completeMatch` had a read-then-write race.** Both devices call it on reaching the verdict; both could pass the `status === 'pending'` check before either wrote, so **both** went on to stop the recording. One stop succeeded and the other came back with Agora's "not recording" error — and the loser's error overwrote the winner's file list, leaving the document claiming zero files for a recording that had produced ten objects. The settle is now claimed in a **transaction**, so exactly one caller proceeds.
    2. **The file list trusted Agora's stop response.** That response only exists when *our* stop request is the one that ended the recording — but Agora's own `maxIdleTime` ends it once the channel empties, and the watchdog can end it too. The list is now read from **Cloud Storage**, which is authoritative in all three cases because the bucket is ours and the files are already there. An "already stopped" error is no longer treated as failure at all: if the files exist, the recording succeeded.
  - **A test recording is deliberately left in place** at `match_recordings/09KQgj1NJx0Vf5iWxhSb/` so the composite framing/quality can be eyeballed in the Firebase console before the editing pipeline is built on top of it.

## Auto-Editing for Highlights — FIRST PASS BUILT (composite/align/mux)
`functions/highlightRender.js` + the admin-only `renderMatchHighlight` callable turn a match's raw per-player HLS into one watchable vertical clip. This was mandatory rather than optional: individual-mode recording produces no directly watchable file, so without it even the human review gate has nothing to review.
- **Output**: 1080×1920 (9:16), 30fps, H.264 + AAC, `+faststart`, both players stacked with the video cropped to fill each half. Verified on a real match — 2:03 of output, ~4 MB, `1080x1920 [SAR 1:1 DAR 9:16]` confirmed by probing the rendered file.
- **The hard part is time alignment, not compositing.** Each player's mic is muted during their opponent's turn, so their audio track has gaps, and the two players start publishing a fraction of a second apart. Feeding the audio playlists straight to ffmpeg concatenates segments end-to-end, collapsing those gaps and sliding every later line progressively out of sync with the picture — which reads as "the video feels off" rather than as an error, and is worst late in the clip where the best material usually is. Every segment is therefore placed at its own absolute offset (`adelay` for audio, `tpad` for a late-starting video), derived from the millisecond timestamp Agora embeds in each segment's **filename** — more reliable than playlist arithmetic, which accumulates rounding error across segments. 18 local tests cover this maths specifically.
- **Rendered on demand, admin-only.** Most matches never become highlights, so auto-rendering every one would burn compute on clips nobody posts. Runs at 4GiB/2vCPU with a 540s timeout — real media work, and `/tmp` is memory-backed on Cloud Functions so the working files count against memory too. A real 2-minute match rendered in ~51s server-side.
- **Three real problems found by running it, not by reading it:**
  1. **`@ffmpeg-installer/ffmpeg` ships a 2018 build** whose `amix` has no `normalize` option, so the filter graph was rejected outright. Switched to `ffmpeg-static` (6.1.1). Note both packages resolve a **platform-specific** binary — a Windows binary locally, Linux at Cloud Build time — which is what makes this work at all given deploys originate from Windows.
  2. **A truncated trailing segment killed the whole render.** Stopping a recording can leave a few-millisecond fragment that ffmpeg cannot even demux. Segments under 8KB are now skipped with a warning recorded on the match, because one unusable scrap must not cost the entire clip.
  3. **Filter input indices were computed from the args array's length**, which advances by two per input (the `-i` flag plus the path), so every filter referenced 0, 2, 4… and ffmpeg rejected the graph with "Stream specifier ':v' matches no streams". Pinned by a regression test.
- **`storage.rules` denies clients read AND write on `match_highlights/**`**, same as the raw footage — and it matters more here, since a rendered clip is watchable and therefore far more attractive to leak, while still being unreviewed. `highlight.reviewStatus` starts `pending` and `published` starts `false`.
- **Deploy note that contradicts an earlier assumption in this doc**: `renderMatchHighlight` initially returned a **Cloud Run-level 401** (Google's generic HTML page, not the function's own JSON) because the "allow public invocation" IAM binding wasn't applied. **Simply redeploying the same function fixed it** — no console access needed. Worth trying a redeploy before assuming the manual console fix is required.
- **BURNED-IN CAPTIONS — BUILT** (`functions/captions.js`), satisfying the Production quality bar's "important for social algorithms and muted autoplay viewing" requirement. Burned into the picture rather than shipped as a sidecar subtitle track, because TikTok/Reels/Shorts don't render external subtitle files.
  - **Speaker attribution is free here, and that's a direct payoff of individual-mode recording.** Captioning a two-person conversation normally needs speaker diarization, which is error-prone and degrades exactly when people talk over each other — which in a roast battle is the interesting part. Because every audio file belongs to one player by construction, every transcribed word already knows who said it, with no guessing.
  - **Google Cloud Speech-to-Text**, chosen for the same reason as Cloud Vision: same GCP project, no new vendor, shared billing and auth. Needed a **one-time manual API enable** (`speech.googleapis.com`), same class of console step as Cloud Vision, Storage and the Realtime Database before it. Each ~15s audio segment is transcribed with the **synchronous** recognizer, which its ~1 minute limit comfortably allows — so no long-running jobs or intermediate GCS uploads. `profanityFilter` is explicitly **off**: masking would mangle the captions of the actual jokes, and the content policy is deliberately permissive.
  - **Word-level timestamps** (`enableWordTimeOffsets`) are essential, not a nicety — without them captions can only be placed per-utterance and drift badly. Each segment's word times are shifted by that segment's offset into the clip's timeline.
  - **Cue style is tuned for short-form, not broadcast subtitling**: at most 4 words held at most 2.5s, split on any pause over 0.6s, each cue held a beat past the last word. Full-sentence subtitles ask the viewer to read while the joke lands. Rendered large and bold with a heavy outline, vertically centred so on a stacked layout the text sits on the seam between players rather than over either face, and clear of the platform UI that crowds the bottom of the screen. **Per-speaker colour** (white / amber) so a viewer can track who's talking.
  - **Verified**: caption burn-in confirmed by extracting real frames from a rendered clip and inspecting them — correct text, correct per-speaker colours, and `{braces}` correctly neutralised rather than parsed as ASS override tags. 17 local tests cover cue grouping and ASS formatting, including the centisecond rounding that would otherwise emit an invalid `.100` timestamp.
  - **NOT verified: transcription of real speech.** The emulators publish silence, so a real recorded match transcribes to zero words — the API call succeeds with no failures, there is simply nothing to hear. **This needs one match on physical devices with real microphones to confirm.** Everything downstream of the words (grouping, styling, burn-in) is verified with injected cues.
  - **Degrades rather than fails**: a segment that can't be transcribed costs only its own captions, and a match with no intelligible speech still renders — uncaptioned, with a warning recorded on the match.
- **TWO RENDITIONS FROM ONE RECORDING — BUILT.** Every render produces both shapes and stores them under `highlight.renditions`, keyed by name, so the social pipeline and the website each take what they need from the same match document:
  - **`vertical.mp4` — 1080×1920 (9:16), players STACKED**, for TikTok/Reels/Shorts.
  - **`landscape.mp4` — 1920×1080 (16:9), players SIDE BY SIDE**, for the website. Side-by-side is genuinely right here and genuinely wrong in vertical (where it gives each player a 540×1920 sliver), and it crops each player *less* than the vertical stack does — roughly 37% of height against 50%.
  - **Transcription runs ONCE and is reused for both.** Speech-to-Text bills per audio minute, so transcribing per rendition would double the cost for identical words. Only the subtitle file is rebuilt per shape.
  - **Caption sizing is a fraction of canvas height, not fixed pixels** — the same cues render at 96px in the 1920-tall vertical clip and 54px in the 1080-tall landscape one. A fixed size legible in one is absurd in the other.
  - **Verified locally on a real match**: both shapes rendered from one source, side-by-side confirmed by inspecting an extracted frame, and the font scaling confirmed in the generated subtitle files. A test asserts the tiles tile exactly — two tiles must fill the canvas in each rendition's stacking direction, leaving no dead space.
- **NOT built yet** (each is a separate pass over the same source): silence/dead-air trimming, branded intro/outro, and background music. **Retention now covers rendered clips too**: the daily sweep deletes `match_highlights/` alongside `match_recordings/` for any match past the window whose highlight was never published. A render is as much a copy of two people's faces as its source, so leaving it would have quietly outlived the retention window the Privacy Policy promises.

- **Rank-change announcements — BUILT and verified.** `functions/rankChange.js` holds the full copy set (10 ranks × 2 directions, the seed examples from the Ranking System section kept where they were good), plus a push on change and an in-app popup on next open.
  - **KEYED ON THE DESTINATION RANK PLUS DIRECTION, not on the pair.** The original design describes pairs, but a rating swing can skip a tier and pairs would need ~90 entries — most never seen, while the ones that DID fire would be the unwritten ones. Twenty lines cover every possible transition including skips, and each is still specific to where you landed. A test walks every from/to combination and asserts none is missing.
  - **The GOAT displacement case is said honestly**, as the design asks: GOAT is the one rank that is a live top-five position, so it can be lost without losing a match. That player gets "You did not get worse. Someone else got better." rather than a decline message. `syncGoatTier` now returns exactly who it displaced so this can actually fire — without that the only honest line in the set was unreachable.
  - **Announced ONCE, after the GOAT sync.** A single finalize can move someone up a tier and then into the top five; notifying at each write site would push twice for what the player experiences as one promotion. Comparing against `lastNotifiedRankTitle` coalesces it.
  - **Two fields, deliberately**: `lastNotifiedRankTitle` (push) and `lastSeenRankTitle` (in-app popup). They legitimately differ — the push goes out while the app is closed, the popup waits until it is next opened — and sharing one would let the push silently swallow the popup.
  - **A brand-new account is never congratulated for existing.** The first read records the title silently, so "you are now Average Joe" never fires for merely signing up, which would devalue every real promotion after it.
  - **THE COPY AND THE LADDER ORDER ARE SERVED, NOT DUPLICATED.** The client asks `getPendingRankChange` rather than comparing the two fields itself, because doing it locally needs the rank order (to tell up from down) and all twenty lines. **The first attempt did exactly that and drifted immediately — the hand-copied order omitted Headliner, which would have called a promotion a demotion for anyone near it.** It also guarantees the push and the popup say the same thing about the same event. Asking is what marks it seen, so it fires exactly once.
  - **Verified against the deployed backend**: a first look announces nothing but records the title, a promotion returns the up voice, asking again returns nothing, a demotion returns the down voice, a multi-tier skip lands on the right message, displacement returns the honest line, and an unranked account is left alone. 8 live checks plus 11 local tests.
  - **STILL OPEN**: multiple variants per transition, which the design asks for to avoid repetition. One line each today, so a player bouncing between two ranks sees the same two lines.

- **ONE STATUS LADDER — a parallel "career title" track was built and REMOVED at the developer's direction. Do not propose another.** The idea was a second ladder of titles earned by accumulating points (Walk-In → Two-Drink Minimum → Road Dog → …), sitting beside the rank titles. The developer rejected it on sight: people "already can earn titles with ratings", and "having two titles seems annoying to keep up with for both the player and dev."
  - **For the player** a second named ladder dilutes the first rather than adding to it. If someone is both an Average Joe and a Road Dog, it is unclear which they *are*, which belongs on a profile, or which to mention. The rank titles get their prestige from being the answer to "how good are you", and a competing title makes that answer ambiguous.
  - **For the developer** every parallel system is another set of thresholds to tune, another set of copy to write, and a new class of collision to police. This is a solo project and that overhead is real.
  - **The tell, worth remembering generally**: building it required a test asserting the two ladders' titles never collide. Needing a guard so two systems do not trip over each other is a symptom that the second system is not wanted, not a problem to engineer around. **Prefer deepening the system that exists over adding one beside it.**
  - **What survives is the part that was doing the actual work.** The concern was real — rating falls, and a losing streak is exactly when people stop opening an app. But the answer is not an abstract title, it is that **a loss still earns points and therefore still moves you toward a clip you can post**. Home now shows the spendable balance with a progress bar toward the clip price ("100 more for the captioned cut of one of your battles") rather than a bare count or a title. That converts into something real, which a title never did.
  - **`points` (lifetime) and `pointsBalance` (spendable) both remain in the data model**, but only the balance is shown. The lifetime figure costs nothing, is written by the same transaction, and is the kind of usage data CLAUDE.md's Firebase Analytics decision calls for when tuning the economy.
- **Earning is confirmed real, not schema-only**: points come from `completeMatch` (played), `finalizeMatch` (won) and `castVote` (judging), all doubled inside the window. So the 250-point clip is roughly 25 matches, 10 wins, or 50 votes — the concrete numbers to react to if that price feels wrong.
- **Points economy — WHAT REMAINS OPEN.** The developer asked to "brainstorm HARD" on what people would genuinely want to earn and spend points on, and this has not been settled. Cosmetics are ruled out (see below). Candidates raised, roughly in order of strength: **points buy a highlight render** (the strongest — it is the exact thing people want, it costs real money so points have genuine backing, and it is the free-tier on-ramp to the flagship paid feature); **points buy tournament entries** (direct line from grinding to prizes, but mint carefully once entries carry cash value); **career points as a permanent parallel ladder** (rating goes down, points only ever go up — "rank = how good you are now, career points = everything you have ever done", with its own all-time leaderboard and volume-earned titles distinct from skill-earned rank titles); **merch**; and **extra skips, hard-capped**. Flagged as needing real legal care before building: **raffle/giveaway entries**, since a chance-based prize draw is a different legal regime from the skill-contest framing. Ruled out: anything that boosts a clip's visibility in the feed — grind-for-visibility undermines "the funniest content rises" exactly as paying for it would. **The structural principle: points need a sink that costs real money, or they are monopoly money.**
- **Bio-reveal length — RESOLVED AND BUILT: 10 minutes is now safe, and settable.** The developer chose 10 minutes (or until both tap Ready). The objection was never the length itself — the reveal already ends the moment both players tap Ready, so the maximum only ever matters when one of them *doesn't*, which made a 10-minute window really "how long can an absent opponent hold you hostage before a no-contest". That is now **bounded by presence rather than by the clock**, so the window can be as long as wanted:
  - The `bioRevealSeconds` ceiling went from **300 to 900**, so 600 no longer falls back to the default.
  - `setMatchReady` gained a **heartbeat mode** (`ready: false`), written every 20s by the bio reveal screen, recording `lastSeenAt.{uid}` on the match.
  - **Judged on presence, NOT readiness — this is the whole design.** With a long reveal a player may legitimately sit thinking about their material for minutes without tapping Ready, and ejecting them would silently collapse a ten-minute window back to seconds for anyone whose opponent taps Ready quickly. Only a genuinely silent opponent counts as gone.
  - `releaseUnresponsiveMatch` settles the match as `abandoned` and **spends no skip** — a skip is for declining someone you were offered, and this player was stood up. Nobody is forfeited either: failing to arrive at a bio reveal is not the broken promise that an accepted-then-abandoned challenge is. The player goes **straight back to searching**, since they passed consent and the camera check moments ago.
  - **A real bug was caught while writing the tests**: the no-heartbeat fallback read `match.createdAt` as a plain number, but it is a Firestore `Timestamp`, so it evaluated to `NaN`, fell through to 0 and silently disabled the fallback — meaning a player who never sent a single heartbeat could **never** be released. Precisely the one case the feature exists for. Pinned by a regression test.
  - **Verified against the deployed backend** with three real accounts: a freshly paired opponent cannot be released on, a heartbeat records presence without marking ready, Ready still marks ready, an absent opponent can be left, the match settles as abandoned and never reaches voting, **no skip is spent**, a READY opponent is never released on however quiet, and a non-participant cannot settle someone else's match. 8 live checks plus 12 local tests. **Hit the documented Cloud Run 401 on the new callable again; a plain redeploy fixed it** — and worth noting two checks initially "passed" only because they were also 401ing, so the whole set was re-run after the fix rather than trusted.
- SUPERSEDED note — the developer wants to try **10 minutes** (or until both tap Ready) instead of 60s. **Blocked on one code change**: `matchSettings.js` bounds `bioRevealSeconds` at **300**, so 600 would be rejected and silently fall back to 60. Worth knowing before setting it: the maximum is not a target (the screen already ends the moment both players tap Ready) — it is **how long an AFK opponent can hold you hostage**, and at 10 minutes that is 10 minutes ending in a no-contest, a sixth of the whole Sixes and Sevens window. 3 minutes was the counter-suggestion. It is one live-config value, so it is a good thing to A/B in the beta rather than argue about.
- **Player directory — BUILT, with its safety rules as part of the feature rather than a later hardening pass** (`functions/playerDirectory.js`, `searchPlayers`, plus `PlayerSearchScreen` and an opt-out on the profile).
  - **Why it needed care at all**: everything else in this app pairs people **randomly**, which means nobody can be sought out. A searchable list of names and faces removes exactly that protection, in an app whose premise is insulting strangers.
  - **Four rules, all enforced server-side** because none can be applied by a client query: anyone can **remove themselves permanently**; a **block hides both people from each other in both directions** (hiding only the blocked party would leave the blocker findable, which is backwards — they are the one who asked not to be contacted); banned accounts are never listed; and you never find yourself.
  - **A result carries a name, a face and a rank, and nothing else.** The profile exists to give an *opponent* ammo during a match — hometown, job and the volunteered ammo text are fine for someone you were just paired with and wrong for a stranger who came looking for you. Only the **first** photo is exposed, since CLAUDE.md requires that one to be a clear face shot and handing a whole gallery to a stranger is a different thing. A test asserts each of those fields is absent from the serialised result, so adding one later fails loudly.
  - **Listed by default, with an explicit opt-out** — only a literal `false` hides someone, because every existing account lacks the field and defaulting to hidden would leave the directory permanently empty. The toggle **writes immediately** rather than waiting for Save: somebody switching it off wants to stop being findable now, not when they remember to press a button. It reverts on failure so the switch never claims a state the server does not have.
  - **Search needed a new `usernameLower` field** (Firestore has no case-insensitive or substring search) plus a **backfill for every pre-existing account** — a range query matches only documents where the field exists, so without it every existing player would have been silently unsearchable while looking normal everywhere else. That is the fifth time this project has met the missing-field trap; the backfill is kept at `functions/live/backfillUsernameLower.js` and is idempotent.
  - **Gated from the start**, like the stats card, rather than opened now and restricted later.
  - **A minimum query length of 2**, refused rather than returning everyone: a one-character search is browsing the userbase, which is not what this is for. The client debounces so typing a name is one search rather than one per keystroke.
  - **Verified against the deployed backend** with six purpose-built accounts: an ordinary player is found; an opted-out one is not; a banned one is not; someone I blocked and someone who blocked me are both absent; I never find myself; the card carries only name/face/rank with hometown, profession, ammo text, rating and the second photo all confirmed absent; search is case-insensitive; a one-character query returns nothing; and a player can re-list themselves. **15 live checks plus 12 local tests.**
  - **NOT built**: challenging a specific person from the directory. That needs the Friend Battles invite mechanism, which CLAUDE.md still lists as undesigned, and it must stay **exhibition-only** when it is built.
